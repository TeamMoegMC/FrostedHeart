## 1. Model

The sky is a sphere of radius `6360 km` with an atmosphere up to `6460 km`, seen from `100 m` above snow. Each color channel is one band, centered at `680`, `550`, and `440 nm`.

| Layer | Profile | Source of its numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Air (Rayleigh) | Exponential, scale height `8 km` | Hillaire 2020, Table 1 |
| Ozone | Tent, peak at `25 km`, half width `15 km` | Hillaire 2020, Table 1 |
| Ground aerosol | Exponential, scale height `1.2 km`, asymmetry `0.8` | Hillaire 2020, Table 1 |
| Nanobots | Gaussian, center `25 km`, width `6 km` | Mie theory for rutile spheres, sizes from `design/world-design.md` |
| Ice crystals | Gaussian, center `11 km`, width `1.2 km`, phase function from hexagonal crystals (below) | Ray traced prisms, tables in `datasets/ice_halos` |

`SkyParticleOptics.forDistribution` averages Mie efficiencies over a log-normal size distribution truncated to `50..1000 nm` and over nine wavelengths per band. The refractive index is the rutile formula `n^2 = 5.913 + 0.2441 / (lambda^2 - 0.0803)` with `lambda` in micrometers. Nanobots do not absorb.

`SkyTables` follows Hillaire 2020: a transmittance table (`256 x 128`, `40` steps), a multiple scattering table (`48` altitudes by `32` sun cosines, `20` steps, `64` directions), and a sky view table (`200 x 100`, `30` steps). Every ray also gets `12` steps of its own across each crossing of the ice band, as described under [Ray steps](#ray-steps). Multiple scattering is computed over a black ground. Light reflected back and forth between snow and atmosphere is added afterwards:

```text
L(view)  = L_black_ground(view) + L_ground * G(view)
L_ground = (A / pi) * E_0 / (1 - A * S)
S        = 2 * integral over mu from 0 to 1 of G(mu) * mu
```

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---|
| `A` | Snow albedo | `0.8`, source constant `SkyAtmosphere.GROUND_ALBEDO` |
| `E_0` | Downward irradiance on a black ground: direct sun through delta scaled ice, the sky view table, and single scattering by ice crystals without the diffraction peak (`IceHaloProfile.irradiance` plus `IceHaloMap.irradiance`) | Computed per sky view |
| `G(view)` | Radiance at the camera per unit radiance of glowing ground | Table of `65` view cosines |
| `S` | Spherical albedo of the atmosphere | Computed per settings |

Radiance is relative to a sun of irradiance `1` in each band. Above the air the sun gives about `128000 lux`, so a radiance of `1` is about `1.28e5 cd/m2` (`SkyAdaptation.CANDELA_PER_RADIANCE`). Snow under a sun overhead then has about `32600 cd/m2`, and the clear sky overhead at a sun of `30 degrees` about `5700`, as real ones do. The shader displays it as `1 - exp(-exposure * L)`, encoded to sRGB, with the exposure adapted to the sky's luminance (see [Adapted exposure](#adapted-exposure)).

### Ray steps

The ice layer is only `1.2 km` wide, so even steps over a ray to the top of the atmosphere put one or two samples in it, about `3.3 km` apart looking straight up. `SkyRaySteps.fill` therefore splits each ray where it enters and leaves the ice band, the shell between `SkyAtmosphere.ICE_BAND_LOW_RADIUS` and `ICE_BAND_HIGH_RADIUS`, four widths below and above the layer's center (`6.2..15.8 km`). Each crossing of the band gets `12` equal steps. Outside it the steps are the cells of the even grid over the whole ray, cut at the band's edges, so the sky without ice samples as before. The transmittance table, the multiple scattering table, `G`, and the view rays all use it. The rows of the multiple scattering table are spaced evenly below the band (`8` intervals), inside it (`16`), and above it (`23`), because the light inside a thick ice layer changes over a few hundred meters. The rows of the transmittance table are spaced evenly in the distance to the horizon, `sqrt(r^2 - R^2)`, below the band (`24` intervals), inside it (`64`), and above it (`39`), for the same reason. Both tables map rows with `BandRows`.

### Ice crystals and halos

The sky view table holds no single scattering by ice. A second table, the ice view, holds that light per unit phase function, and the ice phase function is applied per sky texel:

```text
L_ice(view) = ice_view(view) * (crystals(angle from sun) + w(row) * diffraction(angle from sun) + map(view, sun elevation))
```

Half of the light ice removes is diffracted around the sun, as Henyey-Greenstein with asymmetry `0.99` (`ICE_DIFFRACTION_FRACTION`, `ICE_DIFFRACTION_ASYMMETRY`), and `w` is the diffraction weight described under [Delta scaled ice](#delta-scaled-ice). The other half passes through the crystals of `datasets/ice_halos/crystals.csv`:

| Crystal kind | Share | Shape (half height over radius) | Orientation | Gives |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Tumbling crystals | `40%` | `1.0` | Random | 22 and 46 degree halos |
| Plates | `25%` | `0.15` | Axis vertical, tilt `0.7 degrees` | Sun dogs, parhelic circle, circumzenithal arc |
| Wobbling plates | `5%` | `0.15` | Axis vertical, tilt `5 degrees` | Wider, fainter sun dogs |
| Columns | `20%` | `2.0` | Axis horizontal, tilt `0.7 degrees` | Tangent arcs, circumscribed halo, parhelic circle |
| Parry columns | `10%` | `1.5` | Axis horizontal, two side faces horizontal, tilt `0.5`, turn `1 degree` | Parry arcs |

The mix was chosen for a rich display. The ice refractive index is `n = 1.3005 + 0.0030 / lambda^2`, `1.3070`, `1.3104`, and `1.3160` at the band centers.

`IceHaloProfile` holds the tumbling crystals and, in a row of its own, the diffraction peak as a function of the angle from the sun, in `3600` bins of `0.05 degrees`, from `scattering_tumbling_crystals.csv`. It is built when a setting changes.

`IceHaloMap` holds the oriented crystals for one sun elevation, in `360` columns of azimuth from the sun (`0..180 degrees`, the display is mirror symmetric) by `184` rows of elevation from `-2 degrees` to the zenith, both in `0.5 degree` bins. `IceHaloFormulas` fills it from formulas, so no rays are traced in game:

- light through side faces lies on the cone around the crystal axis through the sun, at the in-plane deviation of `side_faces.csv` for Bravais' effective index `n' = sqrt(n^2 - a^2) / sqrt(1 - a^2)`, where `a` is the cosine between sun and axis. Light with an odd number of end face reflections uses the sun's mirror image in the end faces;
- Parry columns have a fixed turn, so `IcePrism.trace` follows their light through the hexagon per axis;
- light in through an end face and out through a side face is refracted exactly in 3D, over `360` face directions, or the six faces of a Parry column;
- light from end face to end face is followed through its side face reflections by `IcePrism.traceMirrors`;
- the share of light on each path comes from `paths_<crystal>.csv`, by whole degree of sun elevation, interpolated. Light on other paths is put at the sun, and so is the light mirrored by end faces (`end_mirror`) except for the sun pillar of plates below.

The sun pillar is the light the end faces of plates mirror (`IceMirror`, `IceHaloFormulas.addMirrorPillar`). A face tilted by `(v + s) / 2` mirrors a sun at elevation `s` into a view at elevation `v` in the sun's azimuth, at the grazing angle `g = (v - s) / 2`. With the plate's tilt spread `sigma` (`tilt_degrees` of `crystals.csv`), the mirrored light is a Gaussian around the sun's mirror image at `-s`, of standard deviation `2 sigma` along the vertical and `2 sigma |sin g|` across, times

```text
M(v) = |sin g| R(|g|) / D(s)                                  (IceMirror.pillarStrength)
D(s) = E|sin(s) + t cos(s)| + 1.47 q cos(s),  t ~ N(0, sigma^2)   (IceMirror.crossSection)
```

per unit of the light hitting the plates, where `R` is the slab reflectance of both faces (`IceMirror.slabReflectance`), `q` the half height over radius, `D` the plate's mean area seen from the sun over its end face area (end faces with their tilt, then side faces, `1.47 = 8 / (pi sqrt(3))`). `IceMirror.pillarPhase` evaluates the pillar per steradian, with an extra variance for a blur.

`M` gives the pillar its shape, and the data sets its share. Over the whole sky `M` adds up to `IceMirror.pillarShare`, which for level plates with the sun well above the horizon is `sin(s) R(s) / (sin(s) + 1.47 q cos(s))`. That is `4%` more than the share the preview traces with the sun at the horizon and up to `19%` more at `10 to 15 degrees`, since a slab's reflections inside the plate partly leave through a side face of a real plate. So the traced share, the path class `end_mirror` of `paths_<crystal>.csv`, is spread over the pillar's shape. It is `0.039` at `0 degrees`, `0.143` at `3`, `0.18` at `5`, `0.19` at `8 to 10`, and `0.062` at `30 degrees` for plates. Wobbling plates keep `0.142` with the sun at the horizon, where level plates mirror almost nothing.

Only the part of the pillar inside the map moves out of the sun's bin, bin by bin from Gaussian masses along and across (`IceMirror.gaussianMass`), so the map's total does not change while the sun is in it. The part below `-2 degrees` stays at the sun. With the sun below `-2 degrees`, whose other light is dropped, the pillar inside the map is added. The map's blur widens the pillar afterwards like every arc.

A reflection off a side face maps a direction angle `a` inside the hexagon to `2 * normal angle + pi - a`, so after any number of reflections light moves in one of six direction classes per entry face: odd or even reflection count, turned by `0`, `120`, or `240 degrees`. The exit direction depends only on the class and the exit face, not on where the light entered. `IcePrism` keys its outputs by class and exit face and merges those of one trace call, which cut the points written per map from about `1.1 million` to about `150,000` with the same sampling.

Axes are sampled on a jittered grid (plates, up to `196` axes, tilt step `0.5 degrees`) or every `0.25 degrees` of azimuth (columns, `720` axes), weighted by their projected side area. The side face deviation is stepped every `0.25 degrees`, two steps per column of `side_faces.csv`. End face entries are sampled `32` times per axis, `64` for Parry columns. This sampling is dense enough for `0.5 degree` texels; coarser sampling shows as colored dots and streaks along the arcs. Random numbers come from SplitMix64 seeded per axis, so a map does not depend on timing.

Both are blurred by a Gaussian of `sqrt((0.45 * texel)^2 + (sun radius / 2)^2)` with a sun radius of `0.2665 degrees`, so arcs stay about equally bright as they move across texels. Texels below `0.5 degrees`, and the smooth sky, are blurred as `0.5 degrees`, the size of the map's bins (`IceHaloMap.blurSigma`). Oriented light below `-2 degrees` is dropped, so the map keeps `0.20` (sun at `8 degrees`) to `0.25` (sun at `30 degrees`) of the `0.30` the oriented crystals scatter.

The tables were written by the offline sky preview, as described in `datasets/ice_halos/src.md`.

### Delta scaled ice

Diffraction sends light on within a few degrees of its direction, so for everything but the view toward the sun it is as if the light had passed. `SkyAtmosphere` therefore counts only `(1 - f) * beta_ice` in its scattering and extinction totals, where `f` is `ICE_DIFFRACTION_FRACTION` and `beta_ice` the ice's scattering coefficient, and keeps all of `beta_ice` in `iceScattering`. The transmittance table, `G`, and the view rays use the totals, and the multiple scattering table the diffuse totals described under [Forward scattering in the multiple scattering table](#forward-scattering-in-the-multiple-scattering-table), so the sun's beam, the light between scatterings, and the sky seen through ice lose only the ice's other half. The ice view still holds all of the ice's single scattering, so the diffraction peak still draws the glow around the sun. Three things keep diffracted light from counting twice:

- The sun's square uses the full ice extinction, `T(row) * exp(-f * tau(row))`, where `T` is the transmittance of the row's view ray and `tau` the ice optical depth along it. The light diffracted out of the square is in the glow.
- The glow's single scattering uses delta scaled transmittance, which keeps light that is diffracted again in the beam, so light diffracted `n` times would count `n` times. `SkyTables.diffractionWeights` scales the peak per row by `w = (1 - exp(-f * tau)) / (f * tau)`, which leaves the light diffracted at least once. Toward the sun, the square and the glow then carry `T(row)` between them. Rows that hit the ground take the weight of the lowest row of sky, so it stays smooth where the shader interpolates across the horizon.
- `IceHaloProfile.irradiance` leaves the peak out of the light on the ground, since the direct sun through delta scaled ice already carries it.

### Forward scattering in the multiple scattering table

Apart from diffraction, the crystals still send most of their light on within some tens of degrees of its direction. The tumbling crystals' phase has an asymmetry `g`, the mean cosine of the scattering angle, of `0.547`, `0.542`, and `0.533` in the three bands (`IceCrystalData.tumblingAsymmetry`). The multiple scattering table spreads light evenly in all directions at every scattering. With delta scaled ice alone it sent half of the ice's light back up near the top of a thick layer, where a low sun's beam ends, and too much of what snow reflected under a high sun came back down. `SkyAtmosphere` therefore keeps diffuse totals, `diffuseScattering` and `diffuseExtinction`, with only `(1 - g) * (1 - f) * beta_ice` of the ice, the similarity scaling of van de Hulst. The oriented crystals are taken to scatter like the tumbling ones. The multiple scattering table follows its rays through the diffuse totals, and its sunlight comes through a second transmittance table built from them, so light the ice scatters forward lights deeper layers like the sun's beam. The view rays, the sun's square, the ice view, the light ice scatters once onto the ground, `G`, and `S` keep the delta scaled totals.

Under a high sun the daylight on the terrain stays at about `1.0` at every depth, because the display curve is saturated, and under a sun at `30 degrees` it falls from about `0.80` at depth `1` to `0.75` at depth `3`. Thick ice therefore barely dims a high sun's day, as in the path tracer. A gloomy day before snow would need lower, thicker clouds, which the model does not have.

### Sun

The sun is a square with the size and orientation of the core of vanilla's sun texture, its central `8` of `32` texels, which vanilla draws `60` wide at distance `100`. On the plane one unit in front of the camera its half side is `h = 0.075` (`SkyTables.SUN_HALF_TANGENT`), so it is about `8.6 degrees` across, with two edges along the sun's path and two from north to south. Its radiance spreads the sun's irradiance of `1` over its solid angle:

```text
L_sun(view) = T(elevation) / Omega
Omega       = 4 * atan(h^2 / sqrt(1 + 2 * h^2)) = 0.02237 sr
```

`T` is the transmittance from the camera to the top of the atmosphere at the view's elevation with the full ice extinction, the product over the steps of that row's view ray times `exp(-f * tau)`, as described under [Delta scaled ice](#delta-scaled-ice). `SkyTables.sunRadiance` holds `L_sun` per row of the sky view, built with the tables, and zero for rows that hit the ground. So each texel of the square has the color of its own elevation: as the sun sets, the bottom of the square reddens first and the horizon cuts it off. Below about `4.6 degrees` the shader's fade to the fog color dims it further.

With the default settings and exposure, the table displays white above about `8 degrees`, `(255, 237, 134)` at `4 degrees`, `(245, 148, 37)` at `2 degrees`, `(190, 63, 2)` at `0.5 degrees`, and `(154, 37, 0)` at the horizon. Its transmittance is within `1.6%` of a fine integration with the full ice extinction from `1 degree` up. The diffraction peak and the nanobots' forward scattering already make the sky white out to about `2..3 degrees` from a high sun, and about `230` of `255` at the square's edges, so a high sun shows as a white square with soft edges. From about `10 degrees` down, the square stands out clearly against the sky.

### Moon

The moon stands exactly opposite the sun, at the elevation `-e` of a sun at `e`, and lights the sky from the same tables, since they depend on the light's elevation, the ice depth, and the settings, not on whose light it is. `MoonLight.chooseLight` picks the light every frame: the sun while it stands at or above `moonlightSwitchDegrees` (`s`), and the moon while the sun is below it. Each is dimmed by a scale `k` before the tone map, so the sky is black at the switch and no state needs views for both lights:

```text
k_sun  = smoothstep((e - s) / 3)
k_moon = B * P * smoothstep((s - e) / F)
P      = 10^(-0.4 * (0.026 a + 4e-9 a^4)),   a = 45 * min(phase, 8 - phase)
```

- `e`: the sun's elevation in degrees. `s`: `moonlightSwitchDegrees`, default `-15`. The sun fades out over the `3 degrees` above it (`MoonLight.SUN_FADE_DEGREES`, `MoonLight.sunFade`).
- `F`: `moonlightFadeDegrees`, default `1`, over which the moon fades in after the switch and out before dawn (`MoonLight.moonFade`). `0` switches at once. It was `4` until `2026-11-16`. With the switch at `-15`, that left the sky without either light from about `-13` to `-17`, so the moonlit sky and the moon's glow came late after the twilight. At normal speed the sun sinks one degree in about `3.3` seconds of real time.
- `smoothstep(x) = x^2 (3 - 2 x)` for `x` clamped to `0..1`.
- `B`: `moonlightBrightness`, the full moon's irradiance as a share of the sun's, default `2.5e-6`, the real full moon's, from the magnitudes `-12.73` and `-26.74` in Krisciunas and Schaefer 1991. It shows through the night exposure `nightExposure` (`30000`), see [Adapted exposure](#adapted-exposure). Until `2026-11-16`, `B` was a gain of `0.0125` at the day exposure `6`, chosen by eye in game on `2026-11-15`, and `30000` keeps the product `0.075`, so moonlit nights look as tuned then. With that product, in the thinnest ice and at blue shift `0.5`, the sky about `15..30 degrees` from a full moon overhead displays near `(17, 21, 33)` of `255` and the horizon near `(30, 33, 45)`, estimated from screenshots at twice the product. Twice the product reads as a clear mountain night, and with it the light pillars look faint. The range ends at `1e-4`, so a value saved from before is out of range, and Forge clamps it to `1e-4`, `40` times the default. Remove the line or set the default again.
- `P`: `MoonLight.phaseBrightness`, the brightness relative to the full moon by the magnitude law of Krisciunas and Schaefer 1991 (their eq. 9, from Allen 1976), with `a` the phase angle in degrees. `phase` is vanilla's `getMoonPhase()`, `0` for full and `4` for new, so `P` is `1`, `0.335`, `0.091`, `0.0116`, and `0.0003` for phases `0` to `4`, mirrored for `5` to `7`. The law leaves out the brightening by up to `35%` within a few degrees of full. The phase changes at day time `0`, when the moon is off, so in natural time it never pops.

The moon's light is also tinted per channel by `MoonLight.color`, a linear blend from the moon's own color at `moonlightBlueShift` `0` to the blue of night vision at `1`, default `0.5`. The own color, `(1.20, 1.00, 0.82)` relative to sunlight at `680`, `550`, and `440 nm`, is the lunar reflectance of the ROLO model (Kieffer and Stone 2005) near full moon. The moon reddens away from full, to about `(1.27, 1.00, 0.74)` at a quarter, which is left out. The blue is the chromaticity `(0.25, 0.25)` toward which Jensen et al. 2000, "Night Rendering", shift scotopic vision, as linear sRGB of luminance `1`: `(0.706, 0.990, 1.966)`.

With the moon's direction and side, the halos, the ring, and the moon dogs form around the moon before snow as around the sun. `sunScale` `0` leaves out the sun's square, which would otherwise draw an `8.6 degree` white square at the moon. Vanilla's moon sprite is `40` wide at distance `100`, about `22.6 degrees` across, so the glow near the moon mostly sits under it, while the `22 degree` ring and the moon dogs lie outside.

The switch elevation was measured on `2026-11-15` with a temporary GameTest: the highest sun elevation, in steps of `0.25 degrees` from `-2` to `-14`, at and below which every channel of the sun's sky and fog displays under half an 8 bit step, over directions every `2 degrees` in azimuth and `0.25 degrees` in elevation above the horizon. The last light is the red horizon toward the sun.

| Ice optical depth | Exposure `6` | `12` | `50` |
|---|---|---|---|
| `0.02` | `-9.25` | `-9.75` | `-10.5` |
| `0.5` | `-7.0` | `-7.5` | `-8.5` |
| `3` | `-5.5` | `-6.25` | `-7.75` |

Thin ice keeps the glow longest, and `0.02` is the thinnest the weather makes it (`IceWeatherLaw.CLEAR_MINIMUM_DEPTH`), so the default was `-9.5` while the exposure was fixed. The adapted exposure reaches the night exposure `30000` near sun `-11 degrees`, where the sun's sky still shows, and with the switch at `-9.5` the sun's fade then cut the twilight off from bright red to black within about `1.5 degrees`, seen in the harness on `2026-11-16`. So the default is `-15`: at `30000`, the brightest sky at the switch shows `2.3` steps of red at ice depth `0.02`, `0.09` at `0.5`, and nothing at `3` (`sunSkyIsDarkAtTheMoonlightSwitch`, which allows `3` steps), and the sun's fade from `-12` takes away that last sliver. The moon then lights the sky only while it stands more than `15 degrees` high. At all these elevations the terrain light is already vanilla's night light.

### Stars, the moon sprite, and the night floor

Vanilla still draws its stars and its moon sprite over the calculated sky. While the sky is drawn, `NightSkyDimming` dims them, and the sky has a floor it does not go below:

```text
s_star = sqrt( exp(-d / sin 45) * max(0, 1 - z / Z) )
m_c    = D(R_c(-e)) * smoothstep(sin(-e) / 0.08)
f_c    = decode(N) * M_c / Y(M)
T(t)   = 1 - (1 - t)(1 - f_c)
```

- `s_star`: the factor on all four arguments of vanilla's `setShaderColor` for the stars (`NightSkyDimming.starScale`). Vanilla passes its star brightness `b = getStarBrightness * (1 - rainLevel)` as both the color and the alpha, so the light it adds goes as `b^2`, and the share of starlight that shows is `s_star^2`. `d` is the drawn ice optical depth. A star's light that the ice scatters or diffracts out of its point is lost to the point, so this is the full extinction, taken along a path at `45 degrees` for the whole sky. `z` is the displayed luminance of the sky overhead (`SkyViewSet.zenithDisplayLuminance`): the sky view and the ice view at the zenith, the ice by the tumbling crystals at the angle between the zenith and the light but at least `45 degrees`, so the light's own glare does not count, blended and scaled by the light as the shader does, tone mapped and encoded, without the floor or the weather. `Z` is `starTwilightDisplay`, default `0.25`. So stars fade in as the sky overhead darkens at dusk, are a little dimmer under a full moon (about `0.9` of their light at the default moonlight and night exposure, whose sky overhead displays near `0.05`), come out later at dusk as the exposure rises, and fade out as ice thickens before snow, `57%` of their light at depth `0.4` and `1.4%` at `3`. Vanilla's own dimming by the sun's elevation stays, so with no ice and a black sky overhead the stars are vanilla's.
- `m_c`: the factor on the red, green, and blue of vanilla's `setShaderColor` for the sun and the moon, whose alpha stays `1 - rainLevel` (`NightSkyDimming.moonColor`). `R_c(-e)` is the sun's square's radiance at the moon's elevation, the moon standing opposite the sun at `-e`, from `SkyTables.sunRadiance` blended between the two depths as the shader blends it, and `D` the tone map and the sRGB encoding. So the sprite shows as the sun's square would at its place: full in clear air well above the horizon, dimmer and redder toward the horizon, where the air reddens it, and gone below it. The ice dims it evenly in every channel. In snow, at depth `3` and rain level `0.8`, a moon above about `35 degrees` stays near full and a lower one dims, on top of the alpha `0.2` vanilla gives it in snow, so it shows faintly as the sun's square does. The last factor is the shader's horizon fade. It does not take `moonlightBrightness`, the phase, or the moon's color: the sprite's texture shows the phase, and the sprite stays as bright as vanilla's in clear air. Decided with empireu on `2026-11-16`, so that the moon matches the sun and changes smoothly as snow comes and goes. Vanilla's sun quad has no size while the sky is drawn, so the shared call dims only the moon.
- `f_c`: the floor of the night sky as a linear value per channel (`NightSkyDimming.floorColor`, in `SkyFrame`). `N` is `nightSkyFloor`, default `0.04`, a displayed luminance, decoded from sRGB. `M_c` is the moonlight's color at `moonlightBlueShift` (`MoonLight.color`) and `Y(M)` its luminance, so the floor is tinted like moonlight. `T` lifts each tone mapped value `t` before the encoding, in the shader (`withFloor`, `SkyDisplay.withFloor`), for the sky and for the sky with the sun's square in it, and in the fog color (`SkyViewSet.horizonDisplayColor`), so the sky and the fog meet at the horizon. A black sky shows the floor, and a lit sky takes less of it, at most `f_c` at a display of `0` and nothing at `1`. The daylight on the terrain does not take it, so the terrain keeps vanilla's night light. The floor stands for the gray that eyes see in complete darkness. The night sky's own glow, airglow and starlight of about `2e-4 cd/m2`, displays as about `5e-5` at the night exposure, too faint to show, so it is not modeled.

Both factors are computed once per frame after the draw (`CalculatedSkyRenderer.updateNightSky`) and used only while the sky was drawn in the frame (`CalculatedSkyRenderer.isDrawnThisFrame`). Otherwise vanilla's stars and moon are unchanged.

### Adapted exposure

From sunset to night the sky's luminance falls by about a hundred million, and no fixed exposure shows both: at `6` the sky goes black near sun `-4 degrees` while vanilla's is still orange at `-6`, and `24` washes out the sky at `5 degrees`. The exposure adapts to the sky as the eye does (`SkyAdaptation.exposure`, `CalculatedSkyRenderer.exposure`):

```text
L    = 1.28e5 * max( Y( sum_views w * A_c * k_c ), Y( sum_views w * H_c * k_c ) )
a(L) = 1.03 - 2 / (2 + log10(L + 1))
E    = E_d                                   for L >= L_d
E    = E_n                                   for L <= L_n
E    = E_d * (E_n / E_d)^s,   s = (f(L) - f(L_d)) / (f(L_n) - f(L_d)),   f(L) = ln(a(L) / L)
```

- `L`: the luminance of the sky in `cd/m2` that the eye adapts to. `A_c` is `SkyView.adaptationRadiance`, the sky's diffuse irradiance on level ground over `pi`, which is its mean radiance weighted by the cosine to the zenith: the sky view's irradiance with the glow of the ground's light in the air, plus the ice's light without its diffraction peak (`IceHaloProfile.irradiance` and `IceHaloMap.irradiance`), computed with each view in `SkyTables.computeView`. It leaves out the light itself and its glare. The glow off the snow is about a third of the sky's light at a sun of `30 degrees`. `H_c` is `SkyView.brightestHorizonRadiance`, the sky view at the horizon toward the azimuth of highest luminance among the `64` of the mean horizon radiance, which after sunset is the glow toward the sun. `w` are the blend weights of the four views (`SkyViewSet.adaptationLuminance`, `skyAdaptationLuminance`, `horizonAdaptationLuminance`), `k_c` the light's scale and color, and `Y` the luminance with the weights `0.2126`, `0.7152`, and `0.0722`. The eye adapts to the brighter of the two. Adapted to the sky's mean alone, the glow toward the sun, many times brighter than the mean after sunset, and the fog that takes its color brightened as the sun sank, to a vivid orange at `-6 degrees` over the whole fog toward the sun and red hills under a dark sky, seen in the harness on `2026-11-16`.
- `a(L)`: the key of Krawczyk, Myszkowski, and Seidel 2005, "Lightness perception in tone reproduction for high dynamic range images", the middle gray an eye adapted to `L` sees. `a(L) / L` is the exposure of full adaptation, which gives the shape of the curve. Used alone it would show the whole day equally bright and every night, with or without a moon, alike, so it runs only between two limits.
- `E_d`: `exposure`, default `6`, the exposure by day, which holds while the sky is brighter than `L_d`, `adaptationDayLuminance`, default `800 cd/m2`, about the clear sky with the sun at the horizon. So the day looks as before down to sunset.
- `E_n`: `nightExposure`, default `30000`, the eye's limit in the dark, reached when the sky is darker than `L_n`, `adaptationNightLuminance`, default `0.058 cd/m2`. A full moon overhead in clear air gives `0.065`, so it lowers the exposure by about a tenth, to about `27200`. A full moon at `30 degrees` (`0.042`) and the other phases leave it at `E_n`.
- The curve rises steadily from `E_d` to `E_n` as `L` falls and meets both limits without a jump. It has no time course: the outdoor sky changes slowly, and the exposure does not follow the camera into caves or houses, so it changes nothing about how dark they are.

With the defaults, in the thinnest ice, `SkyAdaptationGameTests` gives:

| Sun | `30` | `2` | `0` | `-2` | `-4` | `-6` | `-8` | `-10` | `-12` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `L` in `cd/m2` | `16700` | `2110` | `806` | `176` | `59` | `14.3` | `2.8` | `0.22` | `0.017` |
| Exposure | `6` | `6` | `6` | `35` | `118` | `523` | `2280` | `10900` | `30000` |
| Sky overhead, green of `255` | `134` | `44` | `27` | `22` | `19` | `3` | `3` | `0` | `0` |
| Fog toward the sun, of `255` | `198 194 195` | `134 68 51` | `91 38 33` | `102 41 33` | `112 40 33` | `124 33 12` | `117 26 14` | `76 14 2` | `30 5 2` |

At the fixed exposure `6` the sky overhead would show about `5` at `-2 degrees` and `1` at `-4`, and the fog toward the sun goes black by `-6`. So the glow toward the sun holds its luminance and reddens from sunset to `-8 degrees` and fades by `-12`, and the sky overhead darkens from `-4`. Thick ice brightens a high sun's sky, about twice at depth `3` and `60 degrees`, by forward scattering, and darkens a low sun's a little, measured on `2026-11-16` with a temporary GameTest.

The adapted exposure is the uniform of the sky shader and is used for the fog color, the daylight on the terrain, the sky overhead for the star dimming, and the glints in the light's beam. The moon sprite keeps `E_d`, so it looks as vanilla's in clear air. Lamp glints and light pillars do not use the exposure. The switch `exposureAdapts` off gives `E_d` while the sun lights the sky and `E_n` while the moon does, which with `moonlightSwitchDegrees` at `-9.5` and `moonlightFadeDegrees` at `4` is the output from before the exposure adapted, since the sun's light fades to black above the switch to the moon.

### Light pillars

`LightPillarRenderer` draws light pillars, as described under [Drawing light pillars](#drawing-light-pillars). Their optics are in `IceMirror` and `LightPillarOptics`, for the diamond dust's plate crystals, which float nearly level so that their two large faces mirror a light below them to the eye. Each point of the pillar comes from the crystals at one height. With heights relative to the eye, a light at horizontal distance `d` and height `z_S`, and crystals at height `z`:

```text
tan(e) = (2 z - z_S) / d                  (LightPillarOptics.mirrorElevation, mirrorHeight)
x      = d z / (2 z - z_S)                (crystalDistance, horizontal distance from the eye to the crystals)
s      = 2 sigma sin(e) / (1 + r_2 / r_1)  (lateralSpread, r_2 / r_1 = z / (z - z_S))
```

`e` is the elevation of the point of the pillar and `s` the standard deviation across it on the sky. `sigma` is `tilt_degrees` of the plate crystals in `crystals.csv`, `0.7` for plates and `5` for wobbling plates. A plate reflects `R = F_s / (1 + F_s) + F_p / (1 + F_p)` of the light, from both faces with the light between them, where `F_s` and `F_p` are the Fresnel reflectances of one face for the two polarizations (`IceMirror.slabReflectance`). With the refractive index `1.31`, `R` is `0.944` at a grazing angle of `1 degree`, `0.499` at `10`, `0.217` at `20`, `0.101` at `30`, and `0.035` at `90`, so pillars are brightest at the bottom. Face normals spread as `p(tilt) = exp(-tilt^2 / (2 sigma^2)) / (2 pi sigma^2)` per steradian (`IceMirror.tiltDensity`).

The radiance toward a direction at elevation `e` and lateral angle `psi` from the pillar's plane, per unit intensity `I` of the light and unit face area per volume `a_f`, is the reflection integrated along the view ray. Taking the along tilt `tau` of the face that mirrors the light as the variable, with `beta_S = atan2(z_S, d)` and `q = sqrt(d^2 + z_S^2) sin(e - beta_S)`, the distance from the light to the view ray:

```text
L / (I a_f) = 1 / (2 q) * integral of R(e - tau) p(sqrt(tau^2 + t^2)) d tau
t           = psi (1 + r_2 / r_1) / (sin(e - 2 tau) + sin(e)),   r_2 / r_1 = sin(e - 2 tau + beta_S) / sin(e - beta_S)
```

`tau` runs from `max(-4 sigma, e - 90 degrees)` to `min(4 sigma, (e + beta_S) / 2)`. At `e - 90 degrees` the grazing angle reaches `90 degrees`, and `(e + beta_S) / 2` is the crystals at the eye, which cuts the window at the base of a pillar. `LightPillarOptics.radiance` takes the integral with the `8` point Gauss Legendre rule. Where the window of tilts is short against the ray, it is about the closed form `I a_f R(e) / (2 d) * exp(-psi^2 / (2 s^2)) / (sqrt(2 pi) s)`. That closed form, which an earlier plan proposed, is within `0.2%` for plates from `10 degrees` up, but `27%` too bright where the crystals come close to the eye (a light `300` blocks away and `20` below the eye, at `5 degrees`) and up to `2.3` times too bright at the base of a pillar of wobbling plates, measured outside the game on `2026-11-11` against the integral along the ray. The integral over `tau` removes both, so the base needs no clamp. Brightness falls with `1 / d`, so distant lights still make pillars. The result is `0` where no crystal above both the eye and the light can mirror it, and the face area per volume is taken as the same at every height along the ray.

In the diamond dust, `LightPillarOptics.dustRadiance` takes each of the `8` points of that integral as the crystals at one place along the ray. With `delta = e - 2 tau`, the direction down to the light from those crystals, they are `r_2 = sqrt(d^2 + z_S^2) sin(delta + beta_S) / sin(e + delta)` from the eye, `r_1 = q / sin(e + delta)` from the light, and at height `r_2 sin(e)`. Their face area per volume is `a_f = w beta_0 exp(-max(0, z - z_b) / 80)`, with `w` the plate kind's share in `crystals.csv`, `beta_0` the dust extinction per block, `z_b` the dust's bottom, and `80` blocks its scale height. Their light is dimmed by the optical depth of the dust along both legs, which `dustOpticalDepth` takes exactly for this profile. With the same density everywhere and no extinction, `dustRadiance` is `radiance`. The lateral blur `b`, half the angle of a pixel at the center of the screen (`lateralBlur`), adds `(b k)^2` to the variance of the across tilt, with `k = (1 + r_2 / r_1) / (sin(delta) + sin(e))` from `t = psi k`. That widens the pillar on the sky by `b`, so a pillar narrower than a pixel does not flicker, and keeps its light across it. `light_pillars.glsl` computes the same in single precision.

`LightPillarOptics.BRIGHTNESS`, `433`, turns `dustRadiance` times `a_f` and the light's strength into the value `v` that the pillar adds before its tone curve `1 - exp(-v)`. It is set so that a torch `32` blocks away and `1.12` blocks below the eye, with the dust's bottom `1.62` blocks below the eye at `1 per km`, shows `1 - exp(-v) = 0.1` at `10 degrees`, `0.35` of the display after sRGB encoding, with both plate kinds and the blur of a screen `1080` pixels tall with a vertical field of view of `70 degrees`. Computed outside the game on `2026-11-12`, that torch shows `0.13` in linear light at `1 degree`, `0.22` at `5`, `0.025` at `20`, `0.0079` at `30`, `0.0025` at `45`, and `0.0005` at `80`. At `10 degrees`, a torch `8` blocks away shows `0.24` and one `256` blocks away `0.0098`. The screen size barely matters there: the calibration torch shows `0.097` on a screen `720` pixels tall and `0.102` on one `2160` pixels tall. `pillarBrightness` multiplies it, `0.3` by default, which empireu chose in game.

Each pixel of a pillar shows only the crystals that line up in it, so near pillars glitter and far ones look smooth. A face of area `A` spreads its reflection by diffraction over a solid angle of about `lambda^2 / (A sin(g))`, with `g = e - tau` its grazing angle and `lambda = 550 nm`, so the faces within `lambda^2 / (4 A sin^2(g))` of the mirror direction send the light to the eye. With `a_f / A` crystals per volume, a sky cell one pixel wide holds on average

```text
N = c w beta_0 * integral of exp(-max(0, z - z_b) / 80) p(tilt) r_2^2 / max(sin(g), 0.02)^2 d r_2   (dustCrystalCount)
c = p^2 lambda^2 / (4 A^2),   A = 3 sqrt(3) / 8 D^2                                              (crystalCountScale)
```

crystals, where `p` is the angle of a pixel at the center of the screen and `D` the plates' diameter, `pillarCrystalSize` in micrometers, `65` by default as empireu chose in game on `2026-11-13`. `N` falls as `D^-4`, so `65` micrometers hold about `5.6` times as many crystals as `100`. `LightPillarOptics.dustCrystalCount` takes the integral at the same `8` points as `dustRadiance`, with the near fade and the horizons, and `light_pillars.glsl` sums it alongside the light. Computed outside the game on `2026-11-13` for torches `1.12` blocks below the eye, `100` micrometers, `1 per km`, and a screen `1080` pixels tall, without the near fade, plates show about `14` crystals per pixel at `10 degrees` for a torch `32` blocks away, `2.6` at `20`, and about `1` from `30` to `60`, `0.02` from `10` to `45 degrees` for a torch `8` blocks away, and `7300` at `10` and `115` at `45 degrees` for one `256` blocks away. Wobbling plates have far fewer, `0.2` at `10 degrees` for the torch `32` blocks away.

The fragment stage divides the sky into cells one pixel `p` wide along the lateral angle and the elevation, fixed to the direction of the light, and in each draws a count `k` of crystals from the Poisson distribution with mean `N`. Up to `N = 3` (`LARGEST_COUNTED_MEAN`) it sums the distribution until it passes a uniform chance, for the count of this period and of the next in one pass. Above, it takes a normal deviate `z` by the Box Muller transform and rounds the Cornish Fisher expansion of the Poisson quantile, `k = max(0, floor(N + sqrt(N) z + (z^2 - 1) / 6 + z (1 - z^2) / (72 sqrt(N)) + 1/2))`, which needs no loop. Computed outside the game on `2026-11-14`, the shares it shows are a Kolmogorov Smirnov distance of `0.005` from the Poisson distribution's at `N = 2` and `0.001` at `N = 10`, against `0.177` and `0.083` for a plain normal distribution, which was used above `N = 16` before. Lower down its average drifts above the mean value, by `0.4%` at `N = 2` and `1.3%` at `N = 1.25`. The cell shows `min(k / N, cap)` of the mean value, with `cap = min(64, 1 + 3 / sqrt(N) + 1 / N)` (`sparkleCap`, `MOST_SPARKLE`). The average stays the mean value down to `N = 1/64`, and a cell with fewer crystals shows `64 (1 - exp(-N))` of it, since its rarer and brighter glints are drawn at `64` times the mean. A hash (lowbias32) of the cell's position and its ribbon gives each cell a phase on the twinkle clock, and each period draws a new count and blends to it over the middle half of the period. `LightPillarOptics.sparkle` does the same for the tests. `LightPillarRenderer` advances the clock by the time over `pillarTwinkleSeconds` and by the eye's movement over `0.25` blocks, by at most one period a frame, holds it while the game is paused, and keeps it at `0` while the screenshot harness takes shots. A `pillarCrystalSize` of `0` draws smooth pillars.

## 2. Runtime

| Step | Where | When |
|---|---|---|
| Transmittance, multiple scattering, `G`, `S`, view ray steps, sun radiance | `new SkyTables(settings)` on `Util.backgroundExecutor()` | For ice optical depths on `IceDepthGrid`. The two around the drawn depth are kept, and when the depth leaves them the missing one is built. Both are built again when a setting that rebuilds tables changes |
| Halo profile | `new IceHaloProfile` on `Util.backgroundExecutor()` | On the first build, and when a setting that rebuilds halos changes |
| Halo maps | `new IceHaloMap` on `Util.backgroundExecutor()` | For light elevations on a `0.5 degree` grid from `-6` to `90 degrees`. The two around the light are kept, and when the light leaves them the missing one is built. Both are built again when a setting that rebuilds halos changes, and both at each switch between the sun and the moon |
| Sky views and ice views | `SkyTables.computeView` on `Util.backgroundExecutor()` | For light elevations on a `0.25 degree` grid from `-18` to `90 degrees`, with the tables of each of the two depths. The four around the light and the depth are kept, and when either leaves them the missing ones are built. All four are built again when the tables or the halo maps are, and at each switch between the sun and the moon. At night the moon keeps the halo maps and views building as the sun does by day |
| Upload as `RGB16F` textures | `SkyTextures.uploadTable`, render thread | When a build finishes, only content that no texture holds yet. A view or halo map that moves from the high end to the low end keeps its texture (`reassignSlots`) |
| Draw | `calculated_sky.fsh` on the full-screen quad of `SkyGlPass`, render thread | Every frame while drawn |
| Light pillars | `light_pillars.glsl` on the ribbons of `LightPillarRenderer`, render thread | Every frame with the sky drawn, diamond dust, and a pillar fading in or shown |

What the sky is drawn from in a frame is worked out once, at its first use, in `SkyFrame`: the light's kind, elevation, direction, scale, and color in a `SkyLight`, the sun or the moon as `MoonLight.chooseLight` picks it (see [Moon](#moon)), the ice optical depth from `IceWeatherDepth.update`, the grid indices they call for, the diamond dust extinction from `DiamondDustExtinction.update`, the day exposure, the night sky floor, and the rain and thunder levels. The lightmap's daylight, the fog color, the draw, and `CalculatedSkyRenderer.isSettled` read it. A Forge `RenderTickEvent` at the start of each frame marks it stale, and a call with another partial tick fills it again. The blend weights between views, halo maps, and depths are taken at each use, because a build that finishes in `CalculatedSkyRenderer.render` changes the state after the daylight and the fog color of the same frame used the previous one.

`SkyBuilder` runs the builds, one at a time on one thread, and needs no GL. `CalculatedSkyRenderer.updateTables`, called from `render`, hands each finished `SkyState` to `SkyTextures.upload` and then to `SkyBuilder.install`, which keeps the halo maps and views the new state no longer uses for the next builds to fill. The draw saves and restores the GL state it changes through `SkyGlPass`, draws one static `VertexBuffer` quad, and sets the samplers and constant uniforms once per shader program and the others with raw `glUniform` calls, so its own code allocates nothing per frame. Measured in `runClient` on `2026-11-11` with a temporary probe over many reports of `1000` frames: the draw, the fog color, and the daylight allocated `0` bytes once warm. Some earlier reports showed `24` bytes per draw, likely a mixin callback inside a `GlStateManager` call that the JIT later removes. This was not traced further. The draw uses `12` texture units, which is as many as `GlStateManager` caches: four sky views, four ice views, two halo maps, the halo profile, and one texture with the sun's radiance and the diffraction weights of both depths in four columns. The sky view is mirror symmetric around the sun's azimuth, so only half of it is integrated. `IceCrystalData` reads the datasets once, on the first build, through `ModList.getModFileById("frostedheart")`. Below a sun elevation of `-6 degrees`, which the light reaches only before the switch to the moon, the halo maps stay as they are, since the ice view is dark then.

Each state records the light kind it was built for (`SkyState.lightKind`), and `SkyState.isFor` and `SkyBuilder.request` compare it with the frame's, so a switch between the sun and the moon starts a build. Halo maps and views depend only on the light's elevation, so a build reuses those on the same grid indices whatever their light. The light's radiance is multiplied per channel by `SkyLight.radianceScale`, its scale times its color, which is `0` while the state was built for the other kind. So the sky is black from a switch until the build for the new light lands, as it is at the switch anyway. Other staleness, such as a build lagging the light or a `/time set`, keeps the clamped blend of the state's views. The scale goes in before the tone map in all three places that display the sky: the shader (`lightScale`), the fog color (`SkyViewSet.horizonDisplayColor`), and the daylight (`SkyViewSet.daylightColor`, on the ground radiance but not on the snow it is relative to). The daylight takes `SkyLight.terrainScale`, which is the sun's scale and `0` for the moon, so the moon leaves the terrain at vanilla's night light. The shader's `lightScale` and `sunScale`, which is `0` for the moon, are set every frame.

`LevelRendererMixin.frostedheart$drawCalculatedSky` injects after the first `VertexBuffer.unbind()` in `LevelRenderer.renderSky`, which is right after vanilla draws its sky dome. Vanilla still draws the moon, stars, and the dark dome below the horizon on top. Vanilla's own exits for fog, lava, powder snow, blindness, and darkness still apply. `LevelRendererMixin.frostedheart$tintMoonRed`, `frostedheart$tintMoonGreen`, and `frostedheart$tintMoonBlue` change the color arguments of the third `RenderSystem.setShaderColor` call in `renderSky` (ordinal `2`), which sets the color of the sun and the moon, and `frostedheart$dimStarsRed`, `Green`, `Blue`, and `Alpha` change all four arguments of the fourth (ordinal `3`), which sets the stars' color, as described under [Stars, the moon sprite, and the night floor](#stars-the-moon-sprite-and-the-night-floor). They are `@ModifyArg` handlers, which allocate nothing.

`LevelRendererMixin.frostedheart$hideVanillaSun` replaces the sun's half size `30.0F` in `renderSky` with `0` when the calculated sky drew its sun in the same call (`CalculatedSkyRenderer.isSunDrawn`), so vanilla's sun quad has no area. The flag is cleared at the start of every `CalculatedSkyRenderer.render` and set after a successful draw, so in every case below where the calculated sky is skipped, vanilla draws its sun. Oculus `1.7.0` hides the sun for shader packs that turn it off by emptying the vertex builder before `BufferBuilder.end()`, and Embeddium `0.3.31` injects only at the head of `renderSky`, so neither conflicts with the constant.

The shader:

- snaps each pixel's view direction to the center of its sky texel. Texels are the cells of an equi-angular cube map aligned with the world axes, with `round(90 / texelDegrees)` cells along each face edge, so they stay fixed to the sky as the camera turns, like the sun's texture. Minecraft's sun moves in the x-y plane, so its path runs down the middle of the `+x`, top, and `-x` faces. With `texelDegrees` `0` it uses the pixel's own direction;
- looks up the sky views and the ice views by azimuth from the sun and by the latitude mapping of Hillaire 2020, as `SkyView.sample` does, and blends the two around the sun by its exact elevation, then the two depths by the exact ice optical depth (`IceDepthGrid.blendWeight`). Without the sun blend, each new view shifted the glow around a low sun by up to `16` display steps of `255` every `0.25 degrees`. With it, the sky stays within `1` step of a view computed for the exact elevation, measured halfway between two views at sun elevations from `1.4` to `30 degrees` and ice optical depths `0.1` and `0.3`;
- adds the ice view times the halo profile at the angle from the sun and the two halo maps, blended by the exact sun elevation between their two elevations. The profile's diffraction peak is scaled by the diffraction weight at the row of the sky texel, each depth's weight times its own ice view before the depth blend, so the result is the blend of the two depths' whole radiance;
- takes the sun's radiance at the row of the sky texel, blended between the two depths, times the part of the pixel the sun's square covers, and keeps it apart from the sky until the end. The outline uses the pixel's own direction, with one pixel of smoothing, so the sun glides with sharp edges like vanilla's sun quad, while its color follows the sky texels. Edges on sky texels had to step one texel about every `1.7 s` or fade whole rows of texels in and out, which looked like the square changing shape;
- lifts the tone mapped sky by the night sky floor before the encoding (`withFloor`);
- grays the color by rain and thunder level with the formula of `ClientLevel.getSkyColor`, without the lightning flash, so the sky darkens like vanilla's;
- fades the color toward the fog color by the rain level. In snow `FogModification` sets the fog color, so the sky becomes the pack's snow fog. `PlayerWeatherCompatibilityModel` sets the rain level to `0.8` in snow and blizzards, so a fifth of the sky stays, with faint halos, as a fifth of vanilla's sun does;
- fades to the fog color from the horizon up to a texel direction with `y = 0.08`, about `4.6 degrees`, so terrain fog meets the sky without a seam;
- adds the sun's square over that, times `1 - rainLevel` and the horizon fade, as vanilla adds its sun with alpha `1 - rainLevel` (`LevelRenderer.renderSky`). What it adds is `mix(D(sky + sun) - D(sky), D(sun), rainLevel)`, with `D` the display of a radiance through the tone map and the sRGB encoding. In clear weather that is what the square adds to the sky's radiance, so the sky is the same as with the square inside it, at the horizon fade too. In snow, at rain level `0.8`, a saturated square adds about `0.16`, near vanilla's `0.2`, so the sun shows through snow as vanilla's moon does. Inside the sky's radiance it was lost there, because at the ice depth of snow the glow around the sun is as bright as the square, which then adds nothing to it;
- adds dithering of half a display step against banding, one value per texel so texels stay flat.

`CalculatedSkyEffects` replaces the overworld `DimensionSpecialEffects` through `RegisterDimensionSpecialEffectsEvent`. While the sky is drawn, `getSunriseColor` returns `null`, which removes vanilla's sunrise fan and its tint of the fog color (`FogRenderer.setupColor`). Otherwise it returns the vanilla value. Its `adjustLightmapColors` lights the terrain, as described under [Daylight](#daylight).

### Fog color

`FogRendererMixin.frostedheart$useCalculatedSkyFogColor` injects into `FogRenderer.setupColor` before its call to `getPriorityFogFunction`. While the calculated sky is drawn and the camera is not in a fluid, it sets the fog color to `CalculatedSkyRenderer.fogColor`, which comes from `SkyViewSet.horizonDisplayColor`:

- the sky view table at elevation `0` in the azimuth the camera looks. It has no single scattering by ice, so the color stays smooth near the sun and across halo arcs;
- blended toward `SkyView.meanHorizonRadiance`, the horizon averaged over `64` azimuths, by `1 - h`, where `h` is the length of the look vector's horizontal part, so looking straight up does not pick a direction at random;
- blended between the four views around the light and the ice depth, scaled by the light as the sky is (see [Moon](#moon)), and then tone mapped, lifted by the night sky floor, and encoded, and grayed by rain and thunder as the shader does. So at night the fog is the moonlit horizon, and on a moonless night the floor.

This replaces vanilla's color from sky, biome fog, sunrise, render distance, and weather, which it computes before that call. Vanilla still applies what comes after: darkening for blindness, darkness, the void below `minimum height + 32`, and bosses, then night vision. `ViewportEvent.ComputeFogColor` fires after that, so `FogModification.renderFogColors` still sets its snow fog color whenever its fog density is above zero. Embeddium redirects the biome fog sampling earlier in `setupColor`, and Oculus injects at its tail, so neither conflicts. The fog color is also the clear color behind the world and the color the shader fades to at the horizon.

The calculated sky is skipped, and vanilla draws the sky as before, when:

- `CalculatedSky.enabled` is false;
- the level is not `minecraft:overworld`;
- an Oculus shader pack is in use (`IrisApi.isShaderPackInUse`, only called when Oculus is loaded);
- the camera is inside a fluid;
- no table has been uploaded yet, for the first second or so after enabling;
- building or drawing threw an exception, including a missing or broken dataset. It is logged once, and turning the sky on again with the command retries. Without the command, only a restart does.

### Ice optical depth from the weather

With `iceFollowsWeather` on, the default, `IceWeatherDepth.update` sets the ice optical depth every frame from `IceWeatherLaw`, so cirrostratus thickens ahead of snow and halos foretell it. Hours are climate hours, `50 s` each at normal speed, and snow includes storms.

| Weather | Target depth |
|---|---|
| No snow within `24` hours before or `6` hours after | `0.02 + 0.13 h / 50` (`IceWeatherLaw.clearDepth`), from the day's humidity `h` (`0..50`, `WorldClimate.getHumidity`) |
| `12` hours before snow | `0.4` |
| `6` hours before snow | `1` |
| When snow starts and while it snows (`ClientClimateData.climate`) | `3` |
| `6` hours after snow ends | the clear depth again |

Between these points the target changes evenly in its logarithm, and when the ramp before one snow and the recovery after another overlap, the larger applies. The drawn depth follows the target over the client's climate clock (`ClientWeatherState.frameClimateSeconds`), closing all but `1/e` of the gap in its logarithm per climate hour (`IceWeatherLaw.smoothedDepth`). The day's humidity jumps at midnight and `/time` jumps the clock, and both become a glide or, for a jump of hours, a change at once. A clock that goes back also sets the depth at once.

The client learns about snow from `FHClimatePacket`, which carries `lastSnowEndHour` and `nextSnowStartHour` as absolute climate hours, or `-1` for none (`ClientClimateData`). The server fills them with `WorldClimate.getLastSnowEndHour` and `getNextSnowStartHour`, which search the `24` hours before and after the current hour, not the current hour itself, in the hourly climate of `dailyTempData` merged with a white curtain that covers the player's chunk, sampled in the middle of each hour. The packet goes out every climate hour, after a jump of the clock, and on login, respawn, and dimension change, so after a relog the depth continues where the weather has it. The forecast reaches every player through the sky, whether or not they have the forecast research.

The sky for a depth between two grid depths of `IceDepthGrid`, `S (exp(0.2 i) - 1)` for `i` from `0` to `21` with `S = 3 / (exp(4.2) - 1) = 0.0457`, is the linear blend in depth of the radiance of the two depths' views. The grid steps by about `0.009` near `0` and by a ratio of `exp(0.2) = 1.22` above `S`, and its last depth is the maximum of `SkySetting.ICE_OPTICAL_DEPTH`, `3`, so the depth while it snows is drawn from the tables of a grid depth. A new end needs tables (about `150 ms`) and two views. In the last `12` hours before snow the ramp crosses a grid depth about every `55..75 s` at normal speed, and before that every `45..160 s` depending on the humidity. The recovery after snow, from `3` to a clear depth of `0.05` in `6` hours, crosses one about every `17 s`. While a build is pending the blend weight stops at the end of the cell, so the depth waits there instead of jumping. With `iceFollowsWeather` off, the depth is `iceOpticalDepth`, drawn through the same grid.

### Diamond dust from the weather

Diamond dust is ice crystals in clear, calm, cold air near the ground, and here it follows each snow. With `dustFollowsWeather` on, the default, `DiamondDustExtinction.update` gives its extinction at the ground from `DiamondDustLaw`, in `1/km`, with the diffracted light counted as transmitted as for the ice layer. `SkyFrame` updates it once per frame. The light pillars and the glints near the eye show it (see [Glints of diamond dust](#glints-of-diamond-dust)). The command's status and the light scan below read it, the scan through `DiamondDustExtinction.lastValue`, because an update with an earlier clock than the last would set the extinction to its target at once.

| Weather | Target extinction |
|---|---|
| While it snows or storms (`ClientClimateData.climate`) | `0` |
| From the end of the snow to `1` hour after | rising evenly from `0` to `1 per km` (`DiamondDustLaw.FULL_EXTINCTION`) |
| `1` to `12` hours after | `1 per km` |
| `12` to `24` hours after | falling evenly to `0` |
| No snow in the last `24` hours | `0` |

The hours since snow come from `ClientClimateData.lastSnowEndHour`, as for the ice, and the dust ends at `WorldClimate.SNOW_SEARCH_HOURS`, where the client's knowledge of past snow ends. The extinction follows the target over the client's climate clock, closing all but `1/e` of the gap per climate hour (`DiamondDustLaw.smoothedExtinction`, evenly in the extinction, since the target reaches `0`). A clock that goes back sets it at once. Temperature and wind are not used. Snow stops once the climate temperature rises above `-13` (`WorldTemperature.SNOW_REACHES_GROUND`), and a cold event returns to `0` soon after, about `4` climate hours after the opening storm, so a limit on temperature would cut the dust short. The wind of `WorldClimate` follows only the weather type, settling toward `30` after a blizzard and toward `0` after other snow. With `dustFollowsWeather` off, the extinction is `dustExtinction`.

### Lights for light pillars

`LightPillarSources` finds the lights that can show light pillars, at the end of each Forge `ClientTickEvent`, while the calculated sky is allowed (`CalculatedSkyRenderer.isAllowed`) and the dust extinction of the last frame is above `0`. `LightPillarRenderer` draws the pillars of the chosen lights, as described under [Drawing light pillars](#drawing-light-pillars).

- A pass covers the loaded chunks within the render distance of the camera's chunk (`Options.getEffectiveRenderDistance`, which also takes a server's smaller view distance), in a circle (`dx^2 + dz^2 <= r^2 + r`). `LightPillarRenderer.fillPillars` fades each pillar as its light goes into the terrain fog, as vanilla's `fog.glsl` fades the terrain (`LightPillarOptics.fogDistance`, `fogVisibility`), so pillars end with their lights before the render distance. Each tick tests up to `256` chunk sections and walks the blocks of up to `4` of them, and a pass takes as many ticks as it needs. The heightmap reads of newly reached chunks and two sky light reads per light come on top, so a walked section full of lights, such as lava on the surface, costs more than others.
- Before a column's first section, the scan reads the lowest surface height (`Heightmap.Types.WORLD_SURFACE`, the y just above the top block) of the chunk and the eight around it, each chunk once per pass, and skips the sections whose top is more than `8` blocks below it (`LightPillarClusters.sectionCanShowPillars`). Sky light is at most `15` minus the distance in blocks to open air, so a light there cannot have the sky light a pillar needs. This keeps the scan from walking cave sections with lava, glow lichen, or magma.
- A section is walked only if its palette may hold a block state with a light emission of at least `4` (`LevelChunkSection.maybeHas` with `LightPillarEmitters.showsPillar`), the test vanilla uses in `ChunkAccess.findBlockLightSources`. A light of `3` would show a pillar of `1/25` of a full light's.
- A light's strength per channel is `(emission / 15)^2` times its color, times `min(1, (s - 8) / 7)`, where `s` is the larger sky light (`LightLayer.SKY`) at the light or the block above it. Solid lights such as glowstone count by the air above them, and lights indoors and in caves drop out.
- Lights are merged into one cluster per cell of `4 x 4 x 4` blocks (`LightPillarClusters`), with the summed strength per channel, the center weighted by the summed strength, and a key from the cell's position. A finished pass replaces all clusters of the one before at once, so a light that is placed or removed shows after one or two passes.
- Colors are found once per block state and kept until a resource reload (`FHClientEventsMod.registerReloadListeners`). The override table `datasets/light_pillars/emitters.csv` comes first, by block id and then by tag, as its `src.md` describes. It is read once from the mod file, like the ice datasets, so neither a reload nor a resource pack changes it. Otherwise the color is the average of the block's particle texture (`BlockModelShaper.getParticleIcon`, first frame): opaque pixels, each weighted by the fourth power of its brightest sRGB channel, averaged in linear light and normalized to a brightest channel of `1` (`LightPillarEmitters.averageTextureColor`). Computed outside the game from the vanilla `1.20.1` textures, a torch gives `1.00 0.73 0.32`, a soul torch `0.54 1.00 0.99`, a lantern `1.00 0.62 0.21`, lava `1.00 0.26 0.03`, glowstone `1.00 0.76 0.44`, and a sea lantern `0.85 1.00 0.92`. A texture that cannot be read gives white.
- Every tick, `LightPillarSelection.choose` keeps the `256` clusters (`SLOT_COUNT`) with the highest score, `Y * exp(-max(0, y_c - y_b) / 80) / max(d, 4)`. `Y` is the luminance of the summed strength, `d` the horizontal distance from the camera, and `y_c` the height of the crystals that show the pillar `10 degrees` above its lowest visible point, from `mirrorHeight` with `tan(|beta_S| + 10 degrees)` and `max(d, 4)` for `d`. A light above the eye shows its pillar from its own direction `beta_S` up, and a light below the eye from its mirror image `-beta_S` up, because only crystals above the eye send light down to it. `y_b` is the dust's bottom, which follows the top of the ground under the camera (`Heightmap.Types.MOTION_BLOCKING`) over about `30 s`, so climbing stays in the dust and flying high above it leaves it. After the camera moves more than `16` blocks sideways in one tick, as in a teleport, the bottom is set to the ground at once, and until that ground is loaded no cluster is chosen. `80` blocks is the dust's scale height (`LightPillarSelection.DUST_SCALE_HEIGHT`).
- The `256` slots each hold a cluster by its key, found through a map from key to slot, and fade in or out over `10` ticks, with `fade(slot, partialTick)` between ticks. A newly chosen cluster waits until a slot has faded out. Each tick copies a chosen cluster's lights and shelter (its cap, and the rims and ceilings of `LightPillarShelter`, read for every light when its section is walked) into its slot, so a slot fading out keeps the last ones. A new slot fades in at once. A slot freed at the last tick still has a fade above `0` between ticks, so drawing must go by `fade`, not by `isUsed`. While the scan is idle every slot fades out and a running pass is dropped, so the next pass starts where the camera is. Leaving the level empties everything at once.
- Once warm, the tick allocates nothing: the cluster arrays grow and are kept, the selection has fixed arrays, and the colors are cached. An exception in the scan is logged once and stops it until `/calculatedSky on`.

### Drawing light pillars

`LightPillarRenderer` draws the pillars at Forge's `RenderLevelStageEvent.Stage.AFTER_WEATHER`, in frames where the calculated sky was drawn (`CalculatedSkyRenderer.isDrawnThisFrame`, so not in fluids, fog, blindness, or darkness, where vanilla skips its sky), with a dust extinction above `0`, a known dust bottom, and at least one slot whose `fade` is above `0`. It is one draw call of `light_pillars.glsl`, whose two stages `FHShaders` compiles from that one file with `VERTEX_SHADER` or `FRAGMENT_SHADER` defined, since LDLib's `Shaders.load` reads one stage per file.

- Each drawn pillar has two ribbons, one per plate kind of `crystals.csv`, on one static `VertexBuffer` of quads with `16` segments each, `512` ribbons for the `256` slots. A vertex holds only its ribbon, its row, and its side. `LightPillarRenderer.fillPillars` packs the slots that show into a uniform buffer in slot order, three `vec4` each (the light's position relative to the eye and its count of lights; its color and opacity; its rim and cap toward the eye, its cluster's half width across the view, and its slot), which the vertex shader reads as the std140 block `PillarSlots`, `12 KB` for `256` within the `16 KB` that OpenGL guarantees. Only the ribbons of the packed pillars are drawn, as a prefix of the buffer's indices (`RenderSystem.drawElements` with the type of the shared sequential index buffer). The glitter is seeded by the slot, not the draw order, so it stays while other pillars come and go. The vertex shader lays the ribbon along the pillar, from its lowest elevation (`LightPillarOptics.lowestRibbonElevation`) less one pixel, up to `89.5 degrees` (`HIGHEST_RIBBON_ELEVATION`). Rows are closer together near the bottom: row `i` is at `e_0 + (89.5 degrees - e_0) (i / 16)^2`.
- View bobbing moves the eye up to `0.05` blocks sideways and `0.1` down from the camera, by a translation in the projection matrix that Forge passes on (`GameRenderer.bobView`). The base of a pillar comes from crystals as close as half a block, so `LightPillarRenderer.findEyeOffset` finds the eye as the point that the view projection maps to a clip position with only a depth, the third column of its inverse, and the lights and the matrix are moved to it. Placed around the camera instead, the bases of pillars swung sideways with each step while the tops stayed put.
- Every vertex stands `1` block from the eye in its direction, since the ribbons only cover the pillar on the screen. The scene hides pillars through its depth in the fragment shader, not through the depth test, because the crystals that light one pixel of a pillar spread over many blocks along its view ray, often from near the eye out to about half the light's distance. When each row stood at the crystals with the face closest to level and the depth test hid it, the pillar showed on a wall between the eye and the torches, seen in `runClient` on `2026-11-12`. The crystals of its lower part are in front of the wall, and nothing kept the wall from letting the torches' light reach them.
- A row's half width on the sky is `min(8, sqrt(2 ln(u / v_min)))` times the widest lateral spread among the reflections at that elevation (`widestLateralSpread`), plus one pixel, and at most `80 degrees`. Each row takes the largest half width of itself, the rows next to it, and the half rows between them, so the straight edges between rows hold the pillar too. `u` is the pillar's value `v` at its center in its brightest channel times its opacity, and `v_min` (`VISIBLE_VALUE`) is the value that encodes to half a display step. The pillar falls off sideways at least as fast as a Gaussian of that spread, so outside its ribbon it adds less than half a step. The widest spread is at most about `2 sigma`, so the `80 degree` cap is reached only at `8` spreads of the wobbling plates, which needs `u / v_min` above `e^32`. Where the center is below `v_min`, the ribbon narrows to nothing, so dim pillars and the faint upper parts of pillars cost little.
- With glitter, the half width is also at least `min(8, sqrt(2 ln(N / N_g)))` spreads, where `N` is the mean count of crystals per pixel at the center and `N_g` (`leastVisibleCount`) the mean count below which a cell shows the glint value `v_g` (`GLINT_VALUE`, eight display steps) with less than `RIBBON_GLINT_CHANCE`, `1%`, per twinkle period. Across the pillar the count falls with the value, so each crystal shows about the same value `u / N`, and `N_g` follows from how many crystals it takes to reach `v_g`: the count to that power over its factorial for one or two, the Wilson Hilferty approximation of the Poisson distribution from three, and no less than `sparkleCap` and `64` times the mean allow. A crystal counts as reaching `v_g` from `10%` below it on (`RIBBON_GLINT_MARGIN`), since the count it takes jumps where one crystal shows just `v_g`, and the value of one crystal changes along the pillar between the half rows whose widths the ribbon takes. The smooth pillar is still held down to `v_min`. So at the outer edges of glittering pillars, lone glints below eight steps are left out, and just outside a ribbon about `1%` of cells per period miss a glint. That keeps the cost of glitter down: at `75` micrometers, the ribbons of torches `8` to `32` blocks away cover `1.11` times the sky of smooth ribbons, against `2.56` times when every glint was held down to `v_min` at `0.2%`, as the first version did, and `1.63` times at four steps and `0.2%`, computed outside the game on `2026-11-13`. By the same computation over the lights above at `50` to `200` micrometers, a pixel just outside a ribbon shows `v_g` with a chance of at most `0.87%` away from its ends and `4.8%` at its top and bottom rows.
- With `pillarTexelDegrees` at `0`, the fragment shader computes `dustRadiance` for its pixel's own direction and its ribbon's plate kind, with the lateral blur of half a pixel. Pillars are not snapped to sky texels, because their crystals are at finite distances, so the pillars move against the sky as the eye moves and would flicker from texel to texel. It adds its linear value `v` times the slot's opacity into the pillar light, an `RGBA16F` texture the size of the main target (`GL_ONE, GL_ONE`), cleared each frame pillars draw. A full screen pass (`light_pillars_composite.fsh` over `FHShaders.IMAGE_V`) then adds `encode(1 - exp(-S)) * o` per channel to the frame with a screen blend (`GL_ONE_MINUS_DST_COLOR, GL_ONE`, alpha kept, with the add equation), where `S` is the summed light, `o` the daylight and rain opacity, and `encode` the sRGB encoding. The vertex stage still narrows the ribbons by `o` through the `summedOpacity` uniform, which is `1` where ribbons draw into the frame themselves. Screen blending tone mapped values in linear light equals tone mapping their sum, `1 - e^-a e^-b = 1 - e^-(a + b)`, so overlapping pillars show the tone curve of their summed light and never clip. A single pillar at full fade looks as it did when each ribbon was encoded on its own, and the fades of slots and the fog fade act in linear light. The fill view and debug mode `4` still draw each ribbon into the frame encoded on its own. The target takes `16.6 MB` at `1920 x 1080`. The depth test, depth writes, and culling are off, and there is no fog.
- While `pillarTexelDegrees` (`SkySetting.PILLAR_TEXEL_DEGREES`, default `0.25`) is above `0`, pillars are drawn in square cells of that side on a grid of each ribbon's own (`LightPillarCells`), not of the sky or the screen. Columns go by the lateral angle from the pillar's axis, with column `0` centered on it, and rows by elevation up from the cell origin `max(phi, -phi - 6 sigma)`, the lowest elevation where the pillar can show without blocks around the light. Turning the view leaves every cell where it is on the sky, and moving the eye moves the grid with the light, so cells change their value in place instead of the pillar sliding through them. At `0.25 degrees` a cell is about `3.4` pixels on a screen `1080` pixels tall with a field of view of `70 degrees`, half the side of the sky's default texels.
- A first pass with `CELL_ATLAS` defined (`FHShaders.getLightPillarCellsVertex` and `getLightPillarCellsFragment`) draws the ribbons into the cell atlas, an `R16F` texture `2592` columns wide in bands of rows, each band holding `16` drawn pillars side by side in draw order with `33` columns for the plates' ribbon and `129` for the wobbling plates'. It has as many bands as the most pillars drawn so far need, grown `2` at a time up to `16` and as far as `GL_MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE` allows (`LightPillarCells.grownBands`), and does not shrink while the cell size stays. The cells of pillars beyond its bands are computed at their pixels. Its vertex stage computes the rows' widths as for the screen and places each quad on the atlas with its corners where the screen quad's corners are, one cell further out. The sides of a screen quad lie nearer the horizon than its middle, so the next quad covers the middle above them, and the ribbon's outline reaches one cell further at its bottom and top too. Top vertices carry their side doubled, so the stage can tell them from bottom ones. The scissor test is off while the atlas is cleared and drawn. The fragment stage computes each cell once at its center, without the scene and with the cell's glitter, times the brightest channel of the light's color, which keeps the value within half float range, and leaves the texels of other ribbons alone. The ribbons on the screen then read their cell with `texelFetch`, or compute it at the pixel where the cell lies beyond the atlas, and keep the share of its light from the crystals in front of the scene (`sceneShare`). That share weighs the tilts within the ranges the integral sums by their Gaussian and the near weight alone, with the near weight falling linearly in the tilt between the crystals at the near fade and at its start (`nearTiltMass`) and the normal distribution from a logistic curve (`normalShare`, within `2e-4`), so the scene still cuts pillars at its own pixels. With cells, the margin and the lateral blur follow the cell where it is larger than a pixel, and the crystal count is per cell, so each cell draws its own count. The atlas is cleared each frame, made again when the cell size changes, and deleted at `0`. At `0.25 degrees` it has `420` rows per ribbon and takes about `2.2 MB` per band, `35 MB` with all `16`. Rows are at most `1024`, so with cells below about `0.1 degrees` the tops of ribbons are computed at their pixels.
- The fragment shader reads the main target's depth at its pixel and turns it back into the scene point `P` relative to the eye with the inverse of the view projection. Where the depth is `1`, nothing was drawn and no scene counts.
- Only crystals nearer than the scene distance `t = |P|` count. Along the view ray at elevation `e`, the crystal whose face is tilted by `tau` along the ray is at `s = L cos(e - phi) - L sin(e - phi) cot(2 (e - tau))`, where `L` is the light's distance and `phi` its elevation, which falls as `tau` rises. So `dustRadiance` integrates the tilt only from `max(-4 sigma, e - atan2(L sin(e - phi), L cos(e - phi) - t) / 2)`, which is `e - 90 degrees` with no scene.
- Scene points between the eye and the light, `P . u < d - 0.375` with `u` the level direction toward the light and `d` its horizontal distance, get no pillar light. For every crystal that can mirror the light, the light's way to it passes below the view ray everywhere between the crystal and the light, because `tan e + tan(e - 2 tau) > 0` wherever the crystal sees both the eye and the light. So terrain that the view ray meets before the light, and that is solid below the point it shows, shadows every crystal in front of it. Terrain behind the light shadows nothing, and the pillar shows over it from the crystals in front of it. The `0.375` blocks keep the light's own block and model from shadowing it. Scene points at or above the dimension's cloud height are taken as clouds, which vanilla draws into the main target's depth in Fast and Fancy graphics, and do not shadow.
- The opacity is the slot's fade times `(1 - Y)^4` while the frame's light is the sun, where `Y` is the luminance of the shader fog color as displayed, times `1 - rainLevel`. Pillars fade out through dusk without a threshold and in snow. Under the moon the first factor is `1`, so the moonlit fog does not dim them. The sun's light fades to black above the moonlight switch, so the factor reaches `1` before the light changes. With the fade, a full moon at `moonlightBrightness` `0.025` dimmed the pillars to about `0.43`.
- The pillars draw through a framebuffer of their own that holds the main target's color texture only while they draw, so the main target's depth texture is not attached anywhere it could be written while the shader reads it, as `InfraredViewRenderer` does. In Fabulous graphics this stage runs with the weather target bound, and the main target's depth holds the opaque terrain. The transparency pass then puts translucent terrain, particles, weather, and clouds over the pillars. In Fast and Fancy graphics the depth also holds translucent terrain, particles, and clouds drawn before the weather.
- The program, the draw framebuffer, the blend equation, and the uniform buffer bindings (the generic one, and binding point `8` with its range) are saved before and restored after with raw GL calls, and the blend function and switch, the depth test, culling, the viewport, and the textures on units `0` to `2` through `RenderSystem`. The ribbon buffer is unbound to `0`, as vanilla's own `VertexBuffer` draws leave it. The slots go through a reused buffer, and the uniform buffer's store is let go each frame before the upload.
- The shader approximates the integral with the `4` point Gauss Legendre rule over tilts within `3 sigma` (`NODE_COUNT`, `TILT_RANGE_SIGMAS` in `light_pillars.glsl`), where `LightPillarOptics` takes `8` points within `4 sigma`, which halves the points. Against `dustRadiance` over `200000` random cases in a scratch probe on `2026-11-14`, in display steps at strengths from `0.5` to `16`: away from the first degree above the light, `32%` of the plates' cases and `2%` of the wobbling plates' cases differ by more than half a step, the worst by `3.2` steps for plates where nothing cuts the window and `5.3` steps for wobbling plates. Leaving out the tilts beyond `3 sigma` trims the faint fringe where a window's cut leaves only those tilts, by up to `25` steps for the brightest plate pillars. Within the first degree above the light every rule, the `8` point rule included, is off by up to about `110` steps.
- The integral at each pixel is written for the GPU: the four sines at each of its points come from the sine and cosine of the tilt by the angle sum formulas, the two Gaussians take one exponential with the dimming for the light and one without it for the count, the dust density's exponential also gives the dust column at the crystal, the light's dust column and the eye's are taken once per pixel, the eye's leg times its mean density is the dust column at the crystal less the eye's over `sin e`, and the slab reflectance takes one division. The count is summed only while glitter is on, and the widest spread only in the vertex stage. That is `3` exponentials per point instead of `8`. Within `0.01` blocks of rise, the mean density takes the density at the crystal times a linear correction instead of a second exponential, which differs from the density at the middle by about `1e-9`. The light's elevation and distance come from the vertex stage. With the `8` point rule within `4 sigma` that the shader had then, a port of the shader's math to Java matched `dustRadiance`, `dustCrystalCount`, and `widestLateralSpread` within `5e-10` over `200000` random cases on `2026-11-13`, and in single precision its errors matched the old shader's (median `6e-7`).
- `SkyDrawTimer` measures the CPU time of the draw and its GPU time from two timestamp queries, read back some frames later so the CPU does not wait. Timestamps do not clash with the time elapsed query of Minecraft's own frame timer. Without timer queries, which OpenGL `3.3` and `ARB_timer_query` provide, only the CPU time is measured. `/calculatedSky pillars` prints the means over the last `120` draws. `CalculatedSkyRenderer` has a second instance around the sky's own draw, without the uploads of finished builds, whose means `/calculatedSky` prints as its `Sky draw` line. A timer only reads while its draw runs, so the cost of the sky against vanilla's is the frame time with the sky on and off. Light pillars draw only with the sky, so with diamond dust in the air that difference holds their time too.
- `/calculatedSky pillars fill` switches the fill view (`LightPillarRenderer.toggleFillView`, uniform `fillView`). Every pixel a ribbon covers then adds `0.25` green where its pillar shows at least half a display step and `0.25` red where the fragment stage runs for nothing, so overlapping ribbons add up. Glittering cells turn green only in the periods they glint. With cells, pixels whose cell lies beyond the atlas and is computed at the pixel add `0.25` blue instead.
- `/calculatedSky pillars debug <mode>` sets the debug mode (`LightPillarRenderer.setDebugMode`, uniform `debugMode`), which leaves out one part of the fragment stage to measure its cost with the GPU time. `0` leaves out nothing. `1` shades every pixel whose scene lies beyond the light `0.1` gray instead of computing the integral, so the glitter goes too, but still reads the scene depth. `2` leaves out the glitter's draws and its count sum but keeps the ribbons as wide. `3` neither binds nor reads the scene depth, so pillars show through the scene and pixels behind it compute the integral too. `4` shades every pixel a ribbon covers `0.1` gray and computes nothing. The vertex stage is the same in every mode, so the ribbons cover the same pixels. The mode is a uniform, so the compiled shader and its register use stay those of the full shader. With cells, `1` writes `0.1` as every cell's value instead of computing it, `2` leaves the glitter out of the cell values, and `4` also leaves out the atlas pass.
- An exception in the draw is logged once and stops drawing until `/calculatedSky on`.

### Glints of diamond dust

`DustGlitterRenderer` draws the crystals of diamond dust near the eye as glints, small soft sprites that catch the light for a moment while they fall. It draws at Forge's `RenderLevelStageEvent.Stage.AFTER_WEATHER`, in frames where the calculated sky was drawn, with a dust extinction above `0`, a known dust bottom (`LightPillarSources.dustBottomY`), a rain level below `1`, `dustGlitterCount` above `0`, and either `dustGlitterBrightness` above `0` with a light whose beam reaches the eye or lamps that light glints (below), so lamp glints show also on a night with no moon. `DustGlitterField` holds the field's math in plain Java, which `diamond_dust.glsl` repeats, and whose two stages `FHShaders` compiles from that one file as for the light pillars.

- One `glDrawArraysInstanced(GL_TRIANGLE_STRIP, 0, 4, dustGlitterCount)` with an empty vertex array and no vertex data: an instance of four corners per point. `gl_InstanceID` is the point and `gl_VertexID` the corner. The vertex stage hashes the point (`lowbias32`, `DustGlitterField.hashOf`) to its numbers, and every corner computes the same glint.
- Each point lives one life after another, each `P = 4096 / c` seconds long, with `c` its whole count of lives per wrap of the clock: `2048` up to `4096` (`LEAST_LIFE_CYCLES`, `LIFE_CYCLE_RANGE`) times the life rate `0.2 / dustGlitterLitSeconds` (`lifeRate`, at most `1`), rounded down. A hash of the point and the life's number (`DustGlitterField.lifeHash`) makes each life a new crystal: a start in a cube of `32` blocks (`CELL_SIZE`) that repeats over the world, a fall at `dustGlitterFallSpeed` times `0.7` to `1.3`, tilted from straight down by an angle uniform from `0` to `30 degrees` toward a uniform azimuth (`DustGlitterField.velocity`), and one lit window of `L = k (0.17 + 0.16 h + 0.42 h^12)` seconds (`litSeconds`) that starts at a hashed second of the life and ends within it (`litStart`), with the time scale `k = dustGlitterLitSeconds / 0.2` (`timeScale`, at least `1`). The light rises and falls over `0.05 k` seconds at the window's ends (`LIT_RAMP_SECONDS`, `envelope`), so a glint is above half its peak for `L - 0.05 k`, `0.6` to `3.5` times `dustGlitterLitSeconds` with quartiles `0.8`, `1`, and `1.25` times it. At `0.2 s`, lives last `1` to `2 s` and lit times are those measured on a video of real diamond dust, quartiles `0.16`, `0.2`, and `0.25 s`. The default of `1 s` was set by empireu in game, where `0.2 s` read too short; lit times then run `0.6` to `3.5 s` and lives last `5` to `10 s`. A new life starts somewhere else while the glint is dark, so no glint lights twice in one place. Since lit windows and lives stretch together, at any moment about `17%` of lives are above half their peak (`E[L - 0.05 k] E[c] / 4096`) and `21%` are lit at all, whatever the setting.
- The clock follows time, advances at most `0.1 s` a frame, stands still while the game is paused, and stays at `0` while the screenshot harness takes shots, so frames repeat. The CPU passes its share of the wrap of `4096 s` in two parts computed in double precision, a whole number of `1 / 4096` steps (`clockHigh`) and the rest (`clockLow`), since the product of the first with `c` is exact in float. The life's number `k` and its age then come out within about a microsecond (`lifeNumber`, `lifeAge`), and the life that spans the clock's wrap keeps its number, so nothing jumps there.
- A glint's position is its start plus its velocity times its age, less the eye's offset in its cube, which the CPU passes in double precision, and it shows at the copy nearest the eye (`nearestOffset`). Within a life it moves on a straight line, smoothly at any frame rate. A glint stays where it is as the eye moves and passes by it.
- Glints fade out between `12` and `16` blocks from the eye (`FADE_START`, `FADE_END`), so they are gone before the nearest copy switches at half the cube. About `52%` of the points lie inside the `16` block sphere.
- A point is drawn while its own hashed number lies below `min(1, k / 1)`, with `k` the dust extinction in `1/km` (`DustGlitterField.extinctionShare`). The drawn points grow and shrink as a nested set, so none flickers as the extinction glides. Above the dust's bottom `y_b` a glint's light thins as `exp(-max(0, y - y_b) / 80)`, with `80` blocks the scale height of the light pillars (`DustGlitterField.heightWeight`). A threshold on that height instead would let glints that fall through it pop in and out.
- The halos show more glints, not brighter ones. `p` is the sky's ice phase toward the glint, as `calculated_sky.fsh` reads it: the halo profile's first row at the angle from the light plus the two halo maps blended by the halo blend, without the diffraction peak, since diffraction makes a smooth glow and not glints. With `s_r = min(1, max(p) / p_r)` (`ringShare`), where `max(p)` is its largest channel and `p_r` the profile's green at `22 degrees` (`ringPhase`), a share `q = f + (1 + m / 2 - f) s_r` of lives shows (`shownShare`), with `f` the setting `dustGlitterPlainShare` and `m = 0.15` (`SHOWN_MARGIN`). A life shows with the weight `clamp((q - h) / m + 1/2, 0, 1)` for its hashed `h` (`shownWeight`), whose mean over lives is `q` between `m / 2` and `1 - m / 2`. Away from the halos, where `max(p)` is a few percent of `p_r`, about `f` of lives show, and on the 22 degree halo, the sun dogs, and the pillar all of them, so at the default of `0.2` the halos hold about five times the glints. A glint's tint is `mix(1, p / max(p), 0.5 s_r)` (`TINT_SHARE`, `tint`): white away from the halos and halfway to the halo's own color on them, since the profile's red, green, and blue peak at slightly different angles and would make bands of saturated dots.
- These choices, the tint, and the near share below are taken where the glint is at the middle of its lit window (`middleOffset`), its position now plus its velocity times the time to that middle. While the eye stands still they hold for the whole window, so a glint falling across a halo's edge neither pops nor changes color. As the eye moves they change, softened by the margin.
- Glints show only where their crystal could have fallen, since diamond dust forms in the open air and falls. A life shows only if, at the middle of its lit window, it is at or above the first open height of its column: the height above the column's highest block that stops falling things, as Minecraft's `MOTION_BLOCKING` heightmap holds it, a block with `blocksMotion()` or a fluid (`DustColumnWindow.stopsCrystals`). Glass, glass panes, iron bars, fences, walls, leaves, ice, water, slabs, and stairs close a column, while carpets, snow layers, torches, rails, cobwebs, and plants do not. So no glints show under roofs, glass, or trees, or in caves, while the air outside a window or a cave mouth still glitters. A glint also shows only while it is in open air itself, so it goes out where it falls onto a roof, as the depth test hides it at the ground, and none falls through a ceiling into the room below. The light is unchanged: a glint in open air takes the whole beam, also in the shadow of a hill or a house, as the terrain shows no shadows either. The switch `dustGlitterOpenAir` turns the test off.
- `DustColumns` keeps the first open heights of `64` by `64` columns centered on the camera's column in a float texture (`R32F`), each column at its world coordinates modulo `64` (`DustColumnWindow`), and the vertex stage reads its glint's column with `texelFetch`. The renderer passes the texel of the window's first column, the camera's column counted from it, the camera's place within its block, and the camera's height, so the shader sees only small numbers (`columnInWindow`, `texelOfColumn`, `isOpenAbove`). The middle of a lit window lies up to about `5` blocks from the glint at the defaults and up to about `29` at the longest lit time and fastest fall, within `30 degrees` of vertical, so it stays within about `31` blocks of the eye sideways, inside the window. A column outside the window reads the window's edge. While the camera's column lies outside the window, or the window holds an earlier level, after a jump or a level change and before the next tick follows it, no glint shows.
- Nothing polls the world for the columns. Each client tick centers the window on the camera's column, and the columns that enter it count as closed until read. A jump of `64` blocks or more, a new level, or the tracking starting closes all of them. A block change whose old and new states differ in `stopsCrystals` marks its column, reported by `LightPillarWorldChanges.onBlockChanged` after the chunk's heightmap was updated, and a chunk that arrives, new or sent again, marks its columns in the window (`ChunkEvent.Load`). A marked column keeps its last height until it is read. Each tick reads up to `1024` marked columns through `ClientLevel.getHeight`, which allocates nothing, so a whole window takes `4` ticks, and the next frame uploads the whole texture (`16 KB`) if a height changed. The columns are tracked while glints can be drawn, the calculated sky allowed, `dustGlitterCount` above `0`, and `dustGlitterOpenAir` on, whatever the dust's extinction.
- Torches, lamps, and other blocks that give light also light glints, by the block light where the glint is now, filtered between blocks, so its light changes smoothly as it falls past a lamp. Block light has no direction, so lamps light the plain share of lives, `shownWeight(f, h)` with the direction weight's own hash, with no halo weighting, and those lives are among the ones the beam shows. The color is vanilla's block light color, computed in the shader from the level with `LightTexture`'s own formula: `c = 1.5 x / (4 - 3 x)` with `x` the level over `15`, giving `(c, c ((0.6 c + 0.4) 0.6 + 0.4), c (0.6 c^2 + 0.4))`, each channel clamped to `1` (`DustLightVolume.blockLightColor`). That is the lightmap's block row at sky light `0` before darkness, night vision, the brightness slider, and the gray floor, warm at middle levels and white next to a lamp. The lightmap texture itself would lose it, since with night vision levels `0` and `14` both reach white. Lamp glints pass the same open-air test and take the same envelope, fade, height weight, size, and near weights as the others, by day and by night.
- `DustLights` keeps the block light of `36` by `36` by `36` blocks around the block corner nearest the camera in a 3D texture (`R8`, `GL_LINEAR`, `GL_REPEAT`), each block at its world position modulo `36` (`DustLightVolume`), a level stored as `17` times itself so the texture reads it as a share of `15`. The vertex stage samples it at the glint's position with the texture's own filter. The renderer passes the texel of the volume's first block and the camera's place counted from that block, so the shader sees only small numbers (`textureCoordinate`). Glints show within `16` blocks of the camera, which sits `17.5` to `18.5` blocks from the volume's first block, and the filter reads half a block farther, so a camera up to `1` block from the one the last tick followed still samples inside the volume and never reads the repeat's seam, where the blocks on either side are `36` apart. The sample point is also clamped half a block inside the volume, so after a faster move glints near `16` blocks, which have faded, read the edge's light. While the camera's block lies outside the volume, or the volume holds an earlier level, lamps light no glint. After a jump or a level change the volume is read in texel order, so lamp glints near the camera can stay dark for up to `8` ticks.
- Nothing polls the world for the light either. Each client tick centers the volume, and the blocks that enter it have no light until read. A jump of `36` blocks or more on any axis, a new level, or the tracking starting clears all of them. Light the client computes, for example after a torch is placed, before the server's packet arrives, comes per section through `ClientChunkCache.onLightUpdate` (`LightPillarWorldChanges.onLightUpdated`), and light from the server, with every chunk that arrives and every light update, per column with masks of its sections (`onLightReceived`). Each marks the section's blocks inside the volume, which keep their last light until read. Each tick reads up to `5,832` marked blocks (`DustLights.READS_PER_TICK`), so a whole volume takes `8` ticks and a step across a block boundary `1,296` reads, one section's light array at a time through the block light storage's `getDataLayerData`, which allocates nothing. A section without an array has no block light. The next frame uploads the whole texture (`46,656` bytes) if a light changed. The light is tracked while glints can be drawn, the calculated sky allowed, `dustGlitterCount` above `0`, and `dustLampGlitter` above `0`.
- Glints grow nearer the eye and shrink farther away. A glint's width is `z = clamp((6 / r)^p, 1/4, 4)` times `dustGlitterSize`, with `p` the setting `dustGlitterSizeSpread` (`SIZE_DISTANCE`, `SMALLEST_SIZE`, `LARGEST_SIZE`, `sizeFactor`), and its light is spread over that width, so its peak falls as `1 / z^2` while its whole light stays. At the default `p = 1.5` a glint is `2.8` times as wide at `3` blocks and `0.35` times at `12`, at `p = 1` twice and half, and `p = 0` draws all at the same size. A sprite's radius is at least `2.4` pixels (`SMALLEST_SPRITE_RADIUS`, `spriteRadius`), a core's standard deviation of `0.6` pixels, so a small glint keeps its light as it moves across pixels; the peak then falls by the actual widening (`peakFactor`).
- Near the eye glints are also rarer and fainter, like crystals out of focus. Within `4` blocks (`NEAR_DISTANCE`) a glint's whole light falls as `1 / b^2` with its blur `b = clamp(4 / r, 1, 3)` (`nearBlur`), and fades out from `2` blocks down to `0` at `1` (`NEAR_FADE_END`, `NEAREST_DISTANCE`, `lightFactor`). Against a glint at `6` blocks, at the default size and size spread and `1080` pixels, its whole light is `1.17` at `3` blocks, `0.65` at `2`, and `0.20` at `1.5`, and its peak `0.15`, `0.04`, and `0.01`, while at `12` blocks its whole light is `0.33` and its peak `2.0`, held to the least radius. Only `25%` of lives may show within `3` blocks (`NEAR_SHARE`, `NEAR_SHARE_DISTANCE`), with the same weight over a share that rises to `1 + m / 2` at `4` blocks (`nearShare`), so beyond `4` blocks every life passes and nothing steps there.
- The light of a glint's peak is `v = g (1 - rainLevel) T e a w_h D(r) n d tint`, with `e` the lit envelope, `a` the fade, `w_h` the height weight, `D(r) = 1 / (1 + (r / 4)^2) / (b z)^2` times the near fade, `n` and `d` the near and direction weights, and `r` the distance in blocks. Each glint is a round sprite whose pixels show `encode(1 - exp(-exposure v s))`, added to the frame with a screen blend (`GL_ONE_MINUS_DST_COLOR, GL_ONE`, alpha kept). The shape `s = exp(-8 q) (1 - q)^2`, with `q` the squared distance from the sprite's center over its squared radius, is a Gaussian core with a standard deviation of a quarter of the radius, tapered to `0` at the rim (`SPRITE_RADIUS_SIGMAS`). A glint whose core saturates shows wider, as a bright glint does to the eye. `g` is `0.5` (`CALIBRATED_BRIGHTNESS`) times `dustGlitterBrightness`. With a brightness of `1` a glint `6` blocks away at its peak, with a `10 degree` sun through ice of optical depth `0.1`, where `T` is about `0.2`, `0.1`, and `0.04` in red, green, and blue, rises `50` of `255` in green over a background of `100`, as glints over trees do in the reference video. The screen blend adds `s_c (1 - b)` to a background `b` in the sRGB framebuffer, so the same glint rises `82` over black, `27` over sky at `170`, and `13` over snow at `215`. At the default brightness of `2` it rises about `68` over `100` and `112` over black. As in the reference, glints show against trees and dark terrain and fade over bright sky and sunlit snow. `T` is the share of the light's beam that reaches the eye, `SkyView.lightTransmittance`, sampled by `SkyTables.computeView` from the camera's height toward the light, blended like the sky's views (`SkyViewSet.lightTransmittance`) and times the light's scale, so the moon's glints follow its phase and color with no extra code. It is delta scaled, so the ice is in it, and `0` while the light is below the eye's horizon. A lamp adds `v_l = l (1 - rainLevel) c(L) w_p e a w_h D(r) n`, with `l` the setting `dustLampGlitter`, `c(L)` the block light color at the filtered level, and `w_p` the plain share's weight, so the pixels show `encode(1 - exp(-(exposure v + v_l) s))`, where `exposure` is the adapted exposure. The lamp's light is in display units, as vanilla's block light is, and does not follow the sky's exposure. At the default `l = 1`, a lamp glint `6` blocks away at its peak in full block light rises about `140` of `255` over black, and at block light `8`, about `6` blocks from a torch, about `90` in red and `60` in blue.
- Below `-2 degrees`, where the halo maps stop, the phase of the oriented crystals is the plates' mirror lobe, `IceMirror.pillarPhase` with the halo map's blur variance on both axes, summed over the two plate kinds with each kind's scale. The halo maps keep that scale (`IceHaloMap.mirrorLobeScale`, the plates' traced `end_mirror` share over `IceMirror.pillarShare`), so the lobe continues the pillar that the map holds above `-2 degrees`. The map's lowest row less the lobe at `-2 degrees` fades out over `1 degree` below it (`DustGlitterField.belowMapPhase`), so the phase meets the map without a step and other arcs end softly. Looking down from a hill toward a low sun shows a subsun of glints.
- Sprites are `max(4.8, z 8 / 2.355 dustGlitterSize h / 1080)` pixels wide for a window `h` pixels tall, about `12` at the default size, `6` blocks, and `h = 1080`, whatever the field of view. `dustGlitterSize` is the core's width at half its peak at `6` blocks. The vertex stage projects the glint's center and moves each corner by half that width in pixels (`halfWidth`, `smallestRadius`, `pixelToClip`), times the center's `w` in clip space, so all four corners keep the center's depth and a glint behind the eye clips whole. The fragment stage takes the corner, passed `noperspective`, as the position in the sprite. Triangles are clipped, so a sprite at the screen's edge shows its visible part.
- The vertex stage stops early: a point not drawn, a life not lit, a glint beyond the fade or within `1` block, and a life the near share leaves out are moved outside the clip volume before any texture lookup. The rest read the heights of their columns at the middle of the lit window and where they are now, those under cover are moved outside, and only lit glints in open air look up the phase. Those the direction weight leaves out are moved outside after it. While `dustLampGlitter` is above `0`, the lives the plain share shows then sample the block light.
- The glints draw into the main target's framebuffer, bound for drawing only, with the depth test on (`GL_LEQUAL`) and depth writes off, so the scene hides them and the light pillars, which read the depth, never see them. View bobbing is in the projection matrix, so the glints bob with the terrain. In Fabulous graphics the stage runs with the weather target bound and depth writes on, so the draw binds the main target and turns depth writes off through `RenderSystem`. Face culling is turned off through `RenderSystem`, since the quads' winding is not kept, and turned on again after if it was on, as for the light pillars. The draw framebuffer, viewport, depth test, function, and mask, blend switch, functions, and equations, program, vertex array, the 2D textures on units `0` to `3`, the 3D texture on unit `4`, bound with raw GL since `GlStateManager` keeps only 2D bindings, and the active unit are saved and restored, and `BufferUploader.invalidate` makes vanilla's next immediate draw bind its own vertex array again.
- A frame allocates nothing. `SkyDrawTimer` measures the draw, and `/calculatedSky dust` prints the points drawn in the last frame, the share the dust draws, the mean GPU and CPU time, and the bytes the draw allocated over the last second. It also prints the columns read and marked by block changes in the last second, the uploads, the CPU time of the column work per tick, the bytes it allocated, the columns waiting, and the window's first column, and the same for the block light: the blocks read and the sections marked by light in the last second, the uploads, the CPU time per tick, the bytes allocated, the blocks waiting, and the volume's first block. An exception in the draw is logged once and stops it until `/calculatedSky on`.
- At the defaults and full extinction, about `2,532` of the `32,768` points lie in a `70 degree` view of `16:9` within `16` blocks, and with a fifth shown, `17%` above half their peak, and the fade, about `60` glints are lit at once in plain directions, five times as dense toward the halos, and fewer than one within `4` blocks.
- The defaults of the glitter settings are the values empireu set in game on `2026-11-15`: brightness `2`, plain share `0.2`, fall speed `2`, size spread `1.5`, lit seconds `1`, count `32768`, and size `3.5`. empireu found the default of `dustLampGlitter`, `1`, a first choice from the arithmetic above, right in game on `2026-11-16`.

### Daylight

`CalculatedSkyEffects.adjustLightmapColors` replaces the sky light in the lightmap of `LightTexture.updateLightTexture` while the calculated sky is drawn. Vanilla's sky light at full sky light is `V = t * (0.95 d + 0.05)`, where `d` is `ClientLevel.getSkyDarken` (`0.2` at night, `1` by day, lower in rain and thunder) and the tint `t` is `d + 0.35 (1 - d)` in red and green and `1` in blue (`SkyView.vanillaSkyLight`). At sky light level `i` it is multiplied by `LightTexture.getBrightness(i)`. `SkyView.daylightColor` gives the color `D` that takes its place:

- `C = encode(1 - exp(-k L))` in each channel, where `L` is `SkyView.groundRadiance` blended between the four views around the sun and the ice depth, `k` is the adapted exposure, and `encode` is the sRGB encoding (`SkyDisplay.encodeSrgb`). `L` is the light that snow of albedo `0.8` sends back, from the sun, the sky, ice, and light reflected between snow and atmosphere, so `C` is the snow as the sky displays it below the horizon;
- `C` is divided by the larger of its brightest channel and `encode(1 - exp(-k 0.8 / pi))`, snow under a sun of irradiance `1` at the zenith, so noon is close to white and no channel passes `1`;
- each channel is raised to at least vanilla's night sky light, `V` at `d = 0.2`, which is `(0.1152, 0.1152, 0.24)`;
- the result is blended toward `V` by the rain level, so terrain in snow and storms is lit as in vanilla, as the sky fades to the fog there.

At the first texel of each lightmap update, `CalculatedSkyRenderer.daylightColor` computes `D`, and the hook adds `0.96 (D - V)` times vanilla's darkening during boss fights to every texel, times `getBrightness(i)`. The factor `0.96` and the darkening are what vanilla applies to the sky light before the hook runs. Block light, night vision, the darkness effect, the brightness slider, and lightning flashes, during which the lightmap stays vanilla's, are unchanged. The hook applies under the same conditions as the sky, and also with the camera in a fluid. It changes only what the client draws, so mob spawning and light levels on the server stay as they are. One lightmap update costs one evaluation of `D` and `256` multiply-adds, without allocating.

`SkySetting.Rebuilds` names what a change rebuilds. `TABLES` rebuilds the tables of both depths, `HALOS` the halo profile and both halo maps, which are blurred by the texel size, and either one the four views, whose ground light uses the tables and the halo maps. The rest is kept, so a change of `texelDegrees` keeps the tables, about `150 ms` each, and a change of the nanobots keeps the halo maps. Settings that rebuild nothing are read every frame. Ice tables are built at the grid depths of `IceDepthGrid` whatever `iceOpticalDepth` holds.

Optical depths are vertical, at `550 nm`. `iceOpticalDepth` is drawn only while `iceFollowsWeather` is off, as described under [Ice optical depth from the weather](#ice-optical-depth-from-the-weather). Likewise `dustExtinction`, in `1/km` at the ground, applies only while `dustFollowsWeather` is off, as described under [Diamond dust from the weather](#diamond-dust-from-the-weather), so turning the switch off means no dust unless a value is set. The spread is the geometric standard deviation of diameter. Narrow spreads give colored skies, and wide ones give white haze. An optical depth of `0.25` at the default size is about `30 Mt` of nanobots worldwide. The texel size is rounded so that a whole number of texels fits along each cube face (`SkySettings.actualTexelDegrees`), and `0` draws a smooth sky. Vanilla's sun texture is about `1 degree` per texel. The exposure also sets the daylight on the terrain: a higher exposure keeps it near its noon brightness until the sun is lower. `exposure` is the exposure by day, and the exposure at dusk and at night follows from the four adaptation keys, as described under [Adapted exposure](#adapted-exposure). Luminances are in `cd/m2`.

The renderer compares the config with the settings of the drawn state every frame (`SkySettings.matchesConfig`, without allocating), so changes from the command apply at once. Edits to the config file while the game runs depend on Forge reloading the file, which has not been tried. The existing key `Weather.skyRenderChanges` is unrelated. Its only reader, `DimensionSpecialEffectsMixin`, is not listed in `frostedheart.mixins.json`.

### Screenshot harness

`CalculatedSkyScreenshots` records and takes sets of screenshots for comparisons during development. Its subcommands are part of `/calculatedSky`, so they exist only while `CalculatedSkyClientCommand.ENABLED` is `true`, and nothing else of it runs without them. Taking a set needs a singleplayer world.

| Form | Effect |
|---|---|
| `/calculatedSky beginSet <set>` | Starts recording `<game directory>/sky-shots/<set>.json`, which is `run/sky-shots` in `runClient`. An existing set is loaded and extended, and a new one starts with the sky off and on as its variants |
| `/calculatedSky pose <name>` | Saves where the player stands and looks, with the day time and weather the client shows, as a pose of the set being recorded. A pose of the same name is replaced. The file is written at once |
| `/calculatedSky endSet` | Ends the recording and releases a held time and weather |
| `/calculatedSky time <ticks>` and `time off` | Holds the client's day time for framing, as a run does, and releases it. The server clock keeps running |
| `/calculatedSky weather <clear\|snow\|storm\|snowfall\|blizzard>` and `weather off` | Holds the client's rain and thunder levels for framing, and releases them back to the levels from before |
| `/calculatedSky screenshots` | Takes the current view at the current time with the sky off and on |
| `/calculatedSky screenshots <set>` | Takes every combination in the set |
| `/calculatedSky screenshots stop` | Ends a run early, keeping the shots taken so far |

Taking a set releases a held time and weather, and leaving the world releases them and ends a recording.

A set is one JSON object. Whatever it leaves out comes from the current view, time, weather, and config. Unknown keys and wrong values are reported in chat and skipped or replaced by the default.

| Key | Value |
|---|---|
| `poses` | List of `{name, yaw, pitch, position, offset, times, sunElevations, sunBranch, weather}`. `position` is `[x, y, z]` in the world and `offset` is `[x, y, z]` from where the run started. Without either the pose is where the run started. Yaw follows Minecraft, so `90` faces west toward the setting sun and `-90` faces east. `sunElevations` is a list of sun elevations in degrees, each turned into the day time from `0` to `23999` that puts the sun nearest it (`SunElevationTimes.dayTime`) and added after `times`. Every elevation between the highest and the lowest occurs twice a day, so `sunBranch` picks `setting` (the default, from noon to midnight) or `rising` (from midnight to noon). The pose's times, with those of its sun elevations, and its `weather` replace those of its group. Recorded poses go here, with the time and weather they were recorded with |
| `times` | List of day times in ticks. `18000 + 24000 * n` shows the moon phase of day `n` |
| `weather` | List of `world` (as the server has it), `clear`, `snow` (rain level `1`), `storm` (rain and thunder level `1`), `snowfall`, and `blizzard`. The last two are the levels the server sends a player in snow and in a blizzard (`PlayerWeatherCompatibilityModel.fromClimate`), rain level `0.8`, and thunder level `0` and `0.8`. A recorded pose gets `snowfall` or `blizzard` for a rain level from `0.5` to below `0.9`. The held weather sets only the client's levels, not the ice depth, which follows the world's weather while `iceFollowsWeather` is on |
| `variants` | List of objects with `name`, `sky`, `defaults`, and any key of `SkySetting`, clamped to its range. A key left out keeps the config from the start of the run, or the source default (`SkySettings.DEFAULT`) when `defaults` is `true`, except that a variant that names the manual value of a switch (`SkySetting.manualValue`) turns the switch off unless it sets it to `true`. So a variant with `iceOpticalDepth` draws that depth unless it also sets `iceFollowsWeather` to `true`. Without variants each shot is taken with the sky off and on |
| `groups` | List of objects with their own `poses`, `times`, `weather`, and `variants`. Each group takes its own combinations, and a list it leaves out comes from the set. Without groups the set is one group |
| `hud` | `true` keeps the HUD and the hand. Default `false` |

Shots run group by group, in the order pose, weather, time, variant. For each shot the harness sets the config without saving the file, holds the day time, the rain and thunder levels, and the view direction on the client every frame, and teleports the player through the integrated server when the pose is somewhere else, flying when the player may fly. It waits at least `1 s`, plus `4 s` after a weather change and `2 s` after a move. It also waits until the player has arrived, the terrain is drawn after a move (`LevelRenderer.hasRenderedAllChunks`, which Embeddium answers from its own renderer), and `CalculatedSkyRenderer.isSettled` reports the tables, halo maps, and views built for the config and sun elevation, and no column of the glints' open air and no block of their lamp light waits to be read (`DustColumns.isReading`, `DustLights.isReading`). After `20 s` it takes the shot anyway and says so in the manifest. An open screen, such as chat or the pause menu, restarts the wait, and pausing on lost focus is off while a run lasts.

The frame is saved at the end of `TickEvent.RenderTickEvent` with vanilla's `Screenshot.grab`, into `screenshots/sky-shots/<set>-<date>_<time>/` as `NNN_<pose>_<time>_<weather>_<variant>.png`. `manifest.tsv` in that folder starts with the set, the world seed, and the window size. It then has one line per shot with the player's position, the sun elevation, the rain and thunder levels, the variant's `sky` and every `SkySetting` in the enum's order, the ice optical depth drawn (`drawnIceOpticalDepth`), the diamond dust extinction drawn (`drawnDustExtinction`), a status (`settled`, `timed out waiting for ...`, or `sky not drawn, ...`), and the frames per second over the wait. When the run ends, the config, the HUD, the weather, the view direction, and the position are restored, and the server's next time update restores the day time within a second.

Only the client's time and weather are held, so the server's clock and climate keep running during a run. The client's climate clock follows the server's, not the held day time, so a variant that follows the weather draws the ice of the world's weather under the pose's sun, and the depth can move between shots. `snow` and `storm` only set the client's rain and thunder levels, so they show vanilla snowfall and the snow fades of the sky and the fog. FrostedHeart's storms from the climate (`ClientWeatherFrame`) need a world that is in a real storm, with `world` weather. The contributor's grid tool `ScreenshotGrid`, which lives with the preview outside this repository, lays a run out with `--manifest`, one row per pose, weather, and time and one column per variant.

## 5. Validation And Performance

`CalculatedSkyGameTests` (namespace `frostedheart_calculated_sky`) checks:

- a published Mie value and the Rayleigh limit;
- the normalization of the Rayleigh and Henyey-Greenstein phase functions;
- the datasets: each side face row and each tumbling table integrates to `1`, the path shares of each row and band add up to between `0.99` and `1` (the tracer drops light still inside a crystal after `40` interactions, at most `0.5%`), and the crystal shares add up to `1`;
- the halo profile's total and the halo maps' totals at sun elevations `30` and `8 degrees` against the offline preview, and that the brightest bin of the sun dogs sits between `0.25 degrees` inside and `1 degree` outside the minimum deviation from Bravais' effective index. The preview still puts the light of `end_mirror` at the sun, so it has no sun pillar, which leaves the totals the same;
- the sun pillar (`mirrorPillar...`): that halo maps from sun `-6` to `30 degrees` hold no negative or infinite value, that the pillar of a sun at `-3 degrees` peaks within `0.75 degrees` of `+3 degrees` and is more than `4` times as bright as `4 degrees` to the side, that `IceMirror.pillarShare` of level plates is within `3%` of the slab formula at `5`, `10`, and `30 degrees` and above `0.1` for wobbling plates at `0 degrees`, that the traced `end_mirror` share is `0.75` to `1` times `pillarShare` from `-3` to `30 degrees`, that `IceMirror.pillarPhase` integrates to `pillarShare` within `3%` with and without an extra variance, and that with all of a plate's light mirrored the map holds it all while the sun is in the map, and the pillar outside the sun's bin is the part of its shape inside the map within `0.5%` of the light;
- the default sky at sun elevations `30` and `8 degrees` against the offline preview, with `0.5 degree` texels. The directions include the sun itself, `2 degrees` from it (the diffraction peak), and `22 degrees` from it (the 22 degree halo). ## 6. Known Limits

- One sky for the whole overworld. Biome sky and fog colors are not used while the sky is drawn, except the snow fog of `FogModification`.
- Cloud color stays vanilla, so it can disagree with the sky, most visibly at sunrise and sunset.
- The daylight is one color for all terrain, as in vanilla, with no side lit by the sun. At the fixed exposure `6` it reached the night light at a sun elevation near `0 degrees`, where vanilla's light reaches its night level near `-6 degrees`. The adapted exposure moves this only a little: in the harness on `2026-11-17` (`terrain-2026-11-17_14-58-39`, pose on the ground) the daylight reached the night light by sun `-4 degrees` with it and by `-2` without it, and from `-4` on the snow near the eye showed the same color with and without it. From sun `0` to `-4 degrees` the exposure rises about `20` times, from `6` to `117`, while the ground radiance falls about `27` times, so the snow as the sky's exposure displays it, `(0.110, 0.082, 0.113)` at `-4`, is already below the night light. Adding the displayed snow to the night light instead of taking the larger was tried on the same day and dropped, since it also brightened every low sun and gave sunlit snow a lavender cast. The night light is vanilla's night sky light, constant and blue. The moon lights the sky and the fog but not the terrain, since `CalculatedSkyRenderer.daylightColor` takes only the sun's light. At twice the default moonlight a full moon high in the sky would otherwise light the terrain about twice as bright as vanilla's night light.
- The exposure adapts at once, with no time course, and only to the outdoor sky. Coming out of a cave the sky does not glare, and going in it does not brighten the cave. The moon sprite does not take the adapted exposure, so it does not glare at night as the real moon does to an adapted eye.
- The fog color is one color per frame, as in vanilla, so at the sides of a wide view the horizon can differ a little from it. It has no light from ice crystals, so toward a low sun it is a little darker than the sky above the horizon fade.
- The fog color is the horizon's at any height, as in vanilla, so all fogged terrain takes the horizon's color. Real terrain a few hundred blocks away takes little light from the air. At dusk this paints the terrain toward the sun with the glow, from high up the whole ground below the horizon. From height `200` at sun `-6 degrees` the fog toward the sun shows `(105, 34, 23)`, and vanilla's `(186, 73, 53)`. Blending the fog toward the ground's radiance (`SkyView.groundRadiance`) by the camera's height would be closer to real terrain. It is not done.
- The sky has one light at a time, the sun or the moon, while the real sky after sunset has both. The sun's light fades out from `-12` to `-15 degrees` and the moon's fades in to `-16`, so on a moonlit evening the sky shows the night floor from about `-12` to `-15` degrees, about `10` seconds at normal speed. There the old switch at `-9.5` showed the moonlit sky (`23..29` of `255` against `12..13`). The moon's glow comes in at once at about `-15.5`. A switch where the twilight and the moonlit sky are about equally bright, about `-12` for a full moon, would make the gap smaller but fades the last red of the twilight earlier.
- Between the two limits the exposure grows as `(a(L) / L)^1.25` with the defaults, a little faster than the sky darkens. So the glow toward the sun displays brighter after sunset than at it, up to about `1.5` times its display at sunset at `-6 degrees`, then about four times dimmer by `-12`. At normal speed the sun sinks one degree in about `3` seconds, so the brightest red lasts about `6` seconds and the red band about `20`. empireu found a sunset in real time good on `2026-11-16`. A curve that holds the display of `L` at `a(L)^0.38` between the limits instead would darken steadily, to `0.92`, `0.85`, `0.71`, and `0.44` of the sunset's display at `-4`, `-6`, `-8`, and `-10 degrees`, with the same limits. It is not used.
- Snow fades the sky toward the snow fog. The sun and vanilla's moon show faintly through it, a high sun or moon saturated and a low one dimmed by the ice. Weather changes only the amount of ice. Its crystals and their halos are the same on every day, and a faint halo shows on most clear days. Thicker cloud and the nanobots do not follow the weather.
- Light on other paths is put at the sun, so what those paths draw is missing: light in through a side face and out through an end face (the circumhorizontal arc of a sun above `58 degrees`) and the faint anthelic arcs of columns opposite the sun.
- The sun pillar is drawn only above `-2 degrees`, and the sky fades into the fog color below about `4.6 degrees`, which has no light from ice crystals. So the sky never draws the subsun, which only the glints of diamond dust show, the pillar of a sun above the horizon shows only above it, from the wobbling plates, and the pillar after sunset fades out toward the horizon. The pillar is about `1 degree` wide, from the plates' tilt across it (`2 sigma |sin g|`) and the halo map's blur, which holds the real sun's disc, so it is far narrower than the `8.6 degree` sun square. Below `-6 degrees` the halo maps stop changing, but the sky toward the sun is near black from about `-4 degrees` on. The moon lights the sky only above `15 degrees`, where its pillar is a faint tail.
- The glints of diamond dust have no shade toward the light. In open air they take the whole beam, also in the shadow of a hill, a house, or a tree when the light is low. A column is closed by what stops falling things, so glass, panes, iron bars, and fences keep glints out although light passes them. The column is chosen at the middle of the lit window and checked again where the glint is, so a glint that drifts sideways under an eave while lit goes out there, and one that drifts out from under it lights up at the eave's edge. In Fabulous graphics, glints in front of glass, water, clouds, or particles are drawn under them. They reach only `16` blocks from the eye, and there is no smooth glow of the dust's halos over the terrain beyond. A glint's whole sprite is depth tested at its center's depth, so a glint just in front of an edge of the scene overlaps it, and one just behind shows none of it. They fall about `2` blocks a second by default, so on screen they move slower than in close-up videos of diamond dust, where the crystals are about a block from the camera: about `260` pixels a second at `6` blocks at `1080` pixels and a `70 degree` view, against about `780` there. The halo, tint, and near choices hold through a lit window only while the eye stands still, so a glint can pop at a halo's edge while the eye moves fast.
- Lamp glints take vanilla's block light, which has one color and no direction. So glints around a soul lantern are warm while its pillar is cyan, a lamp lights glints evenly on all sides with no halo of its own, and light that passes glass or leaves lights glints beyond them. A glint beside a wall takes light filtered toward the wall's `0`. Like all glints they reach only `16` blocks from the eye.
- Light pillars only rise. A layer of crystals below both the eye and a light would show a pillar pointing down, which is not drawn. Pillars end at `89.5 degrees`, and a light less than `0.5` blocks away sideways shows none. Fog does not cover them, and in Fabulous graphics clouds are drawn over them even where the clouds are behind their crystals.
- Terrain shadows pillars only where the eye sees it, and as if it were solid down to the ground. A wall right in front of a light hides the pillar where the eye sees the wall, but the pillar then rises from the wall's top, although the wall would also shadow crystals well above it. Tree crowns, roofs, and other overhangs between the eye and a light hide the pillar where they show, although light can pass under them. Mobs, players, and dropped items in the depth hide pillars the same way, and in Fast and Fancy graphics so do water surfaces and particles. Terrain at or above the cloud height never shadows.
- The glitter of light pillars is fixed to the direction of each light, so as the eye moves the pattern moves with the pillar and the twinkle clock runs faster, rather than each crystal moving past. Each cell is one pixel at the center of the screen, so glints are a little larger toward the edges of a wide view. A cell with fewer than `1/64` crystals on average shows less than its mean value, which dims the parts of near pillars whose crystals are within a few blocks of the eye.
- A light that stands for a cluster sits at the strength-weighted center of the cluster's lights, which can be up to about `4` blocks sideways from some of them, so terrain right at a cluster can shadow its pillar or fail to.
- At most `256` clusters show pillars at once. Where more are in view, as in a big lit town, the dimmest are left out and swap in and out with their fades as the eye moves.
- The light scan reads the light emission of the block state, not Forge's emission by position, so a block that changes its light by position counts with its state's value. It skips lights more than `8` blocks below the lowest surface around their chunk even where sky light reaches them, such as under a glass roof over a deep pit. A light whose particle texture is not the color of its light needs a row in the override table.
- A resource pack's `sun.png` is not used while the calculated sky is drawn, since the shader draws the sun. The pack does not replace it.
- Around new moon the moon gives the sky almost no light, since phases `3` to `5` give at most `1.2%` of the full moon's light, so the sky and the fog show the night sky floor, set by eye. Vanilla's night fog is a very dark blue, `5..8%` of its day fog with the pack's colors. The moon lights the sky only while the sun is below the switch, so a moon lower than `-moonlightSwitchDegrees` lights nothing.
- The stars all take one factor, so a thick layer dims them evenly over the sky instead of more toward the horizon, and the sky's glow around the moon does not hide the stars near it more than elsewhere. The moon sprite takes the sun's square's transmittance through the middle of the sprite, one color for the whole sprite.
- In snow, blindness does not darken the fog, because `FogModification` sets its snow fog color after vanilla's darkening. This does not depend on the calculated sky.
- `runClient` uses vanilla biomes. The `2026-11-06` run used a datapack that gives them the pack's sky and fog colors, but the pack's own world generation has not been tried.
